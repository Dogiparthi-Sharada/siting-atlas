=============================================================================
1. ONE PARAGRAPH FOR THE PROFESSOR
=============================================================================

  "We replaced our demand assumption with a fitted model. We used the US
  DOT's National Household Travel Survey, where real households report how
  many goods were delivered to their home in the past 30 days. We fitted a
  regression of deliveries on income, household size, renting, workers,
  density and age, and tested it on 20% of households it never saw: it
  predicts group averages closely and beats 'everyone gets the average' by
  24% on deviance. We then applied it to every US ZIP using Census data, and
  set the total to Amazon's published 2024 volume from the Pitney Bowes
  Parcel Shipping Index. The survey tells us WHERE demand is; Pitney Bowes
  tells us HOW MUCH. The result: income matters more than we assumed (0.44,
  not 0.35), but station workloads and the cost per parcel ($1.18) barely
  change, so our earlier results stand."

2. THE DATA (all verified, files in sources/)
=============================================================================

  NHTS 2022 (public v2)      7,520 households used (of 7,893)
                             target: DELIV_GOOD = goods delivered, past 30 days
  NHTS 2017                  124,947 households used (of 129,696)
                             target: DELIVER = online purchases delivered
  Pitney Bowes Parcel Shipping Index, 2026 report:
    US parcels 21.7bn (2023), 22.4bn (2024), 23.1bn (2025)
    171 parcels per household in 2025 (= 3.29 a week; matches our old 3.2)
    Amazon Logistics 6.3bn (2024), 6.9bn (2025) - now the largest carrier
    -> Amazon per household per week = 6.3bn / 135m households / 52 = 0.90
  Census ACS (already in the project): 32,828 ZIPs, 128.7m households

  What US households actually report (weighted to the US):
    2022: 8.6 goods deliveries a month = 1.98 a week
    2017: 5.0 online purchases a month  = 1.15 a week   (+73% in 5 years)

=============================================================================
3. THE MODEL
=============================================================================

  log(expected deliveries per month) = b0 + b1 ln(income) + b2 household size
                                       + b3 renter + b4 workers
                                       + b5 ln(density) + b6 adult age

  Type: Poisson regression (the standard model for counts), weighted so the
  sample represents the US.

  Why "log": it makes every effect a percentage. Example: b2 = 0.143 means
  each extra person in the household multiplies deliveries by e^0.143 =
  1.154, i.e. +15.4%.

=============================================================================
4. WHAT IT FOUND (coefficients, 95% confidence intervals)
=============================================================================

                         NHTS 2022 (main)            NHTS 2017 (check)
  Income sensitivity     0.44  [0.40, 0.48]          0.51  [0.50, 0.52]
  Each extra person      +15%                        +11%
  Renter vs owner        -17%                        -13%
  Each extra worker      +10%                        +8%
  Density (log)          ~0  (-0.016)                ~0  (+0.025)
  Each year of age       -0.3%                       -1.8%

How to read them, with examples:
  INCOME 0.44: a household with 10% more income gets about 4.4% more
    deliveries. Doubling income: 2^0.44 = 1.36 -> +36%.
    Our old guess was 0.35. Two independent surveys say 0.44-0.51, with
    tight intervals, AFTER controlling for household size. So the guess was
    too low.
  HOUSEHOLD SIZE: a family of 4 vs a couple: 1.154^2 = 1.33 -> +33%.
  RENTERS: -17%, even at the same income (fewer places to receive parcels,
    smaller homes - our interpretation, not tested).
  DENSITY: almost no effect. City and rural households order about the
    same once income and household size are accounted for.
  AGE: older households order a bit less; the effect was much stronger in
    2017 than 2022 (older people caught up during the pandemic - our
    interpretation, not tested).

5. HOW ACCURATE IS IT? (the hold-out test)
=============================================================================

We hid 20% of households from the model, fitted on the other 80%, then
predicted the hidden ones.

                                        2022              2017
  Deviance explained vs "average only"  24.3%             27.2%
  Mean error, model                     6.15 /month       3.98 /month
  Mean error, "everyone = average"      7.22 /month       4.82 /month
  -> improvement                        15%               17%

Group averages on the hidden households (2022), deliveries a month:

  Model's predicted fifth   lowest   2nd    middle   4th    highest
  Observed                  3.86     5.26   8.33     9.67   16.82
  Predicted                 3.28     5.75   7.59     10.50  15.84

  By income bracket: observed vs predicted within about 1 delivery a month
  for most brackets; the worst is bracket 8 ($100-125k: 12.1 observed vs
  10.4 predicted), and the two lowest brackets (few households) are
  under-predicted by about 1. The 2017 hold-out, with 16 times more
  households, is closer still (section output in fit_output.txt).

IF THE PROFESSOR SAYS "24% is low":
  "For one household, yes; whether a single family orders 3 or 15 times
  this month depends on things no survey records. But we don't use it to
  predict single households. We use it for ZIP averages, and on hidden
  households the group averages line up closely, from about 4 a month in
  the lowest fifth to about 17 in the highest."

7. DOES IT CHANGE OUR RESULTS?
=============================================================================

  Station workloads (Amazon parcels/day; real range 20,000-60,000):
                    median    within   below   above   states(5+) inside
    old formula     21,397    241      219     21      24 of 29
    NHTS model      20,822    231      230     20      22 of 29

  Cost per parcel (median):
    old formula     $1.1808
    NHTS model      $1.1810
    NHTS model with 1.2 parcels per stop: $1.37

  CONCLUSION: the fitted model confirms the old assumption was roughly right
  about WHERE demand is (rank correlation 0.83), corrects the income effect,
  and adds household size and renting. The station check and the cost per
  parcel do not change. The correction that mattered was the LEVEL (all
  carriers 3.2 -> Amazon 0.90), and that is now pinned to a published source.

=============================================================================
8. LIMITATIONS - SAY THEM BEFORE HE DOES
=============================================================================

  1. Self-reported counts: people round and forget.
  2. Deliveries, not parcels: one delivery can be two boxes.
  3. All retailers, not just Amazon: we assume Amazon's share of
     deliveries is the same in every kind of ZIP. That is the biggest
     assumption left.
  4. Learned on households, applied to ZIP medians: a ZIP's "average
     household" is not a real household.
  5. Confidence intervals ignore the survey's complex design (NHTS
     publishes replicate weights we did not use), so they are somewhat too
     narrow. The 2017-vs-2022 agreement is the more convincing evidence.
  6. Timing: Amazon volume is 2024, Census is 2019-2023, survey is 2022.
  7. Delivery days: we divide weekly demand by 6 days, as the cost model
     does. Amazon delivers 7 days a week; at 7, every daily station load is
     14% lower (median about 17,800), which pushes more stations below
     20,000. Worth saying.
  8. Unlabelled codes: the data dictionary has variable names but not
     value labels. Income brackets and the renter code were matched to
     known US figures (renters 30% vs US ~34%; urban 81% vs Census ~80%).
     The NHTS codebook with value labels would confirm them.

9. LIKELY QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS
=============================================================================

"Is this circular, like week 1?"
  "No. In week 1 the target was invented from our own assumptions. Here the
  target is what real households reported to the US DOT. The model learns
  from data we didn't make up, and we test it on households it never saw."

"Why not predict Amazon's demand directly?"
  "No public data has Amazon deliveries per household or per ZIP. So the
  survey gives the pattern across households, and Amazon's published
  national volume gives the level."

"Why should I trust 0.44?"
  "Two separate surveys, five years apart, with 7,500 and 125,000
  households, give 0.44 and 0.51, both after controlling for household
  size. Our old 0.35 was a guess; the published HNS figure measures a
  different thing (a spending ratio)."

"So what was the point, if the cost didn't change?"
  "The point was to stop the cost result resting on a guess. It now rests
  on a tested model and a published Amazon volume, and we can show the
  cost is robust to it."

"What would you do next?"
  "Use the replicate weights for proper confidence intervals, and find a
  source for parcels per stop, which moves the cost more than demand does."
