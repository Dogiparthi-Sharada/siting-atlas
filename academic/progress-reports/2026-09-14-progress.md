# Siting Atlas — progress report

**Date:** 14 September 2026
**Period covered:** 12–14 September 2026
**Status:** the project's year-old binding constraint has been removed. The
prediction model did not improve as a result, and we can now say precisely why.

---

## 1. One-paragraph summary

The project models where Amazon builds last-mile delivery stations using only
free public data, so that communities can evaluate siting decisions they
currently negotiate blind. Its binding constraint for a year was that **no
facility had a known opening date** — every date on record was an upper bound
inferred from a safety inspection. An earlier attempt to recover dates from
satellite imagery failed and was abandoned. In the period covered we recovered
**1,420 opening dates** by OCR of a public industry article, validated them
three independent ways, and used them to expand the analytical panel from 104
to 700 facilities. The expanded sample **sharpened the model's parameter
estimates by 64% but left its predictive accuracy unchanged**, which is the
signature of a ceiling in the covariates rather than in the sample. The same
new dates then made possible an audit that had never been performed: a test of
whether the model's one working covariate contains its own outcome.

---

## 2. What was blocking the project

Every facility in the panel had an **upper bound** on its opening date and no
lower bound. The bound came from OSHA inspection records: an inspector
physically present in a building proves it was operating by that date, but not
when it opened. The panel could say "open by 2019" and never "opened in 2019".

That single gap blocked the lag guard on the industry covariate, blocked any
use of time in the choice model, and made before-and-after designs impossible.

Two routes had been tried. Sentinel-2 satellite imagery was attempted and
**failed**: 36% of its estimates dated construction *after* the inspection that
proved the building was already operating, which is not imprecise but
impossible. It was written up as a negative result and abandoned. Commercial
network data exists but is sold, and the project's premise is free sources.

## 3. What was done

### 3.1 Extraction

MWPVL International publishes a public article documenting Amazon's
distribution network. Its facility tables are **images, not text** — 13 table
images totalling 152,915 pixel-rows, with no machine-readable content.

A pipeline was built to recover them. The technically material decision: plain
OCR was implemented, measured, and **abandoned**, because table rows wrap over
two to four printed lines and reading order splits a date across them — the
month on one line and the year on the next, with no reliable way to rejoin
them. The pipeline instead recovers each word with its pixel coordinates and
reconstructs the table geometrically: columns from horizontal position, rows
from vertical position.

```
  1,904 facilities recovered
    1,767  (93%)  with a postal code
    1,420  (75%)  with an opening year
      873  (46%)  with an opening month
       43         flagged "not confirmed" by the publisher itself
```

By facility class: 635 US small-package delivery stations (the target class),
121 heavy/bulky delivery stations, 385 fulfilment centres, 114 sortation
centres, 69 inbound cross-docks, 458 rest-of-world sites.

### 3.2 Validation — three independent tests

Because OCR errs on digits and digits are the whole point, the extraction was
graded against evidence rather than inspection.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| **Against OSHA.** A building cannot open after an inspector stood inside it. | **93.6%** pass (157 linked, 10 falsified) |
| **Against the network's start date.** The delivery-station network launched in late 2013. | **2 of 790** delivery-station rows predate it |
| **Against an independent geocoding.** The OCR'd state versus the state implied by the postal code. | **99.8%** agreement (556 of 557) |

The second test is the most informative. The parser has no knowledge of
Amazon's corporate history, yet the recovered dates stop almost exactly at the
2013 network launch, while fulfilment centres — a facility class that
legitimately predates it — freely date back to 2008 and earlier. Nothing in the
code enforces that boundary. It holds because the digits were read correctly.

**For comparison, the abandoned satellite method failed the first of these
tests on 36% of its output. This fails on 6.4%.**

### 3.3 Panel expansion

Records were merged into the analytical panel using the project's existing
Fellegi–Sunter probabilistic record linkage, with no new matching rule written
for the occasion.

```
  facilities                104  ->  700
  with a dated opening      104  ->  556
  distinct metropolitan areas 62  ->  230
  usable decisions            94  ->  485
  events per parameter      15.8  ->  76.0     (conventional floor: 10)
```

## 4. Results

### 4.1 More data sharpened the estimate and did not improve prediction

```
  warehousing coefficient (ratio to the numeraire)
    before   1.528  [0.985, 2.550]
    after    1.170  [0.895, 1.455]      64% narrower interval
```

The interval narrowed substantially and **still contains 1.0** — the covariate
remains statistically indistinguishable from the numeraire. The point estimate
moved *toward* 1.0, not away from it.

Predictive accuracy, measured on held-out data over 50 re-samples and reported
separately by market size, because averaging across markets of very different
difficulty measures the mix of questions rather than the model (§4.2):

```
  top-10 lift over chance        original          expanded
    medium markets (26-100)    2.75 (n=41)      3.03 (n=162)
    large markets (>100)       6.18 (n=46)      6.33 (n=257)
```

Both move slightly upward, and neither moves by more than the spread across
re-samples. **Five times the sample bought precision, not accuracy.** This is
the expected behaviour when the limit is the information content of the
covariates rather than the number of observations, and it is corroborated
twice below.

*Source: `outputs/metrics/panel_experiments.json`, 50 re-samples. Small
markets are excluded from the comparison: the original panel holds only 7 such
decisions, too few to compare, and in a choice set that small random guessing
already scores about 67%, so lift is capped near 1.5x however good the model.*

### 4.2 A methodological finding about our own reporting

An earlier reading of the pooled figures suggested performance had *fallen*
(lift 3.68× → 2.61×). That was an aggregation artefact and is worth recording
because it would have led to the wrong conclusion.

The expanded data is weighted toward small markets. Decisions with fewer than
25 candidate ZIP codes rose from 7% to 14% of the sample. **In a choice set
that small, random guessing already lands in the top ten about 67% of the
time**, so the maximum achievable lift is capped near 1.5× regardless of model
quality. Averaging across markets of very different difficulty therefore
measures the *mix of questions*, not the model.

Consequence: pooled top-k across heterogeneous choice sets is not an
interpretable statistic for this problem, and results are now reported
stratified by market size.

### 4.3 A benchmark that settles an open question

A gradient-boosted ranker was built and evaluated on identical splits, over 50
paired re-samples:

```
  conditional logit                 20.60 of 38     best Brier score of all
  warehouse count, nothing fitted   20.96 of 38
  gradient-boosted ranker           22.30 of 38
```

The flexible learner finds something the structural model's functional form
cannot express, and it is worth **about one decision in 38**. The spread
between all methods (1.7) is smaller than any single method's variation across
re-samples (2.4–2.9). **The ceiling is the data, not the model** — with a small
but real functional-form term.

### 4.4 A new covariate class, and the first interval that excludes the numeraire

The extraction recovered 1,269 facilities that are *not* delivery stations —
fulfilment centres, sortation centres, inbound cross-docks. Parcels flow
fulfilment centre → sortation centre → delivery station, so proximity to the
existing network is a theoretically motivated predictor the model had never
had. It was built time-respecting: a decision may only see facilities that
were already open when it was made.

Across five panel compositions and three algorithms, this is the only new
covariate class the project has found whose coefficients sit strictly in the
interior — every other candidate collapses to the boundary, meaning the model
can say only "zero".

```
  sortation_proximity    0.110  [0.007, 0.255]   metro-clustered bootstrap
  fulfilment_proximity   0.164  [0.007, 0.419]   metro-clustered bootstrap
```

Both stay above zero under proper inference, so the covariate class is real.
The effect is small: about **two decisions in 194**.

**A stronger claim was tested and withdrawn, which is worth recording.** An
earlier reading had the key coefficient's interval excluding the numeraire for
the first time in the project's history — a headline result. It does not
survive. Asked five ways:

```
  re-split percentiles        1.401  [1.037, 1.869]   EXCLUDES 1.0
  MLE on all 485 decisions    1.312       —
  bootstrap over 194 metros   1.312  [0.977, 1.920]   contains 1.0
  sandwich, metro-clustered   1.312  [0.961, 1.792]   contains, p = 0.088
  sandwich, independent       1.312  [0.864, 1.993]   contains, p = 0.203
```

**All three estimators that are inference contain 1.0. The one that excluded
it is the one that is not inference** — a percentile spread across re-samples
describes how a number moves when the data are re-split, not how uncertain it
is. The clustered interval is 13% *wider* despite being fitted on 485
decisions rather than 291, which is the cleanest demonstration that the spread
understates.

There is also a measured theoretical cost. A proximity is not an extensive
quantity, so merging two zones does not add their attractions. Over 43,239
merged pairs the relative error is **0.00** for the extensive-only control and
**0.046 median, 0.470 maximum** for this specification. The model remains a
valid conditional logit for a fixed set of alternatives; what it loses is the
*derivation* — the invariance argument that justified the functional form in
the first place.

Predictive gain from the network covariates: about **two decisions in 194**,
improving in 42 of 50 re-samples. Real, small, and consistent with the ceiling
described above.

### 4.5 An audit the new dates made possible

The model's one working covariate is a count of warehousing establishments per
ZIP code. **An Amazon delivery station is itself a warehousing establishment.**
A guard exists to prevent a facility being counted in its own predictor: it
requires industry data measured before the facility's opening year.

The new dates show that guard is defeated. The true opening is a **median 34
months earlier** than the inspection-derived date the guard relies on (n=42;
88% of gaps exceed one year). The "safe" measurement is typically taken *after*
the building opened.

An ablation confirmed the stakes: removing the covariate costs 7.70 of 38
correct predictions and loses on 0 of 50 re-samples. The model is substantially
this one column.

The decisive test refits using, per facility, industry data from strictly
before its **stated** opening date:

```
  contaminated vintage    9.24 of 12
  honest vintage          8.22 of 12      retains 79% of the covariate's value
  covariate removed       4.48 of 12
```

Supporting this, a model-free check: between the two vintages the chosen ZIP
code gains **+1.04 warehouses more** than its rival ZIPs in the same metro —
almost exactly one building, which is what self-counting predicts. But the
median excess is zero and only 11 of 29 facilities show it.

**Conclusion: the covariate is mostly genuine agglomeration, with a
self-counting component affecting roughly a third of facilities and worth at
most a fifth of its predictive value.** The result survives the audit.

*Caveat, stated plainly: requiring both a stated date and a clean industry
vintage reduced the sample to 29 decisions, 69% of the working set. The
honest-vintage arm also reads data a median two years older, so staleness and
contamination are partially confounded and the 21% figure is an upper bound on
the leak.*

## 5. What this is worth

Three things that did not exist a week ago.

**A measured visibility gap.**

```
  cities where MWPVL names a delivery station      459
  cities OSHA has inspected, ANY facility class    340
  in both                                          124
  named by MWPVL, never inspected by OSHA          335

  the best free source sees 27% of them
```

This is a direct two-list intersection — no model, no independence
assumption — as distinct from the capture–recapture estimates the project also
runs, which agree in direction.

Two things make it conservative rather than convenient. MWPVL's side is
restricted to delivery stations while OSHA's side is *every* Amazon building it
ever inspected, so OSHA is allowed to score a match on a fulfilment centre in a
city where MWPVL names a delivery station; restricting OSHA fairly would widen
the gap. And MWPVL is itself incomplete and says so about this very table, so
459 is a floor on the true count. The measured gap therefore understates.

*Source: `outputs/metrics/mwpvl_coverage.json`.*

**A covariate audited for circularity.** Very few applied papers test whether
their key predictor contains its own outcome. This one now does, with a
quantified answer, and it is only possible because the opening dates exist.

**A primary source for the project's central claim.** The same article carries,
unprompted, this from its publisher:

> *"in future we will no longer be updating this information online due to the
> high rates of plagiarism of this content … If there is an interest in
> obtaining current information available in XLSX format then please contact us
> and we can provide details regarding the commercial terms."*

The only free public census of Amazon's US network is being withdrawn, the
stated cause is that it was free, and the same paragraph names the paid
replacement. The project's argument is that the asymmetry between a corporation
and a county planning department is **price, not scarcity**. That is the
argument stated by a party with no interest in making it.

## 6. Open items

| Item | Status |
|---|---|
| Industrial land-use collection (OpenStreetMap) | Running; 62 metros, ~3 hours |
| Testing 5 well-populated panel columns the model has never used | Running |
| Four-arm comparison of panel compositions | Running |
| Full project audit for untracked gaps | Running |
| Expanded panel fails an internal validator (144 rows lack a numeric year) | Open; requires a decision, not a fix |
| 1,269 non-delivery-station facilities extracted but unused | Open |

**The most promising open item** is the industrial land-use data. In a large
metro the model ranks 300+ candidate ZIP codes, but most are physically
incapable of hosting a 150,000 sq ft warehouse. The model does not know this.
An independent finding supports the diagnosis. Forty facilities were ranked by
cost-to-serve **within their own metropolitan area**:

```
  in the cheapest 10% of their metro      0%    (chance: 10%)
  in the cheapest 25%                    12%    (chance: 25%)
  median rank                          0.44     (chance: 0.50)
```

None of the forty sits in its metro's cheapest decile. Under the cost model
used here, cost falls as one over the square root of demand density, so the
cheapest places to serve are the densest — and the densest are exactly where a
warehouse cannot be built. *Feasibility binds before economics.*

This raises a hypothesis worth testing directly: that the warehouse count
predicts well because it is a **buildability proxy** rather than an
agglomeration measure — which would explain both why it is the only covariate
that works and why it carries a circularity risk. A clean land-use measure
would do the same work without the risk.

---

## 7. Honest assessment

The prediction model has not improved and, on present evidence, may not. Two
independent lines now point to a ceiling in the free covariates: a flexible
learner beats the structural model by one decision in 38, and quintupling the
sample changed accuracy not at all.

That is not a failure of execution, but it does mean the strongest contribution
is unlikely to be the prediction. It is more likely to be the **measurement of
what free public data can and cannot see**, of which the coverage gap, the
audited covariate and the withdrawal of the last free source are all parts.

The next fortnight should establish whether a buildability measure breaks the
ceiling. If it does, the prediction result stands on its own. If it does not,
the ceiling itself — quantified across several methods and covariate sets — is
the finding, and it is a more defensible one than a model that merely worked.
