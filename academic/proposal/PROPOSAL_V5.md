## Executive Summary

Between 2023 and 2026 major retailers restructured national fulfilment networks
into regionalised, node-based architectures capable of sub-24-hour delivery. Each
ZIP-code area enrolled in same-day service carries a $3-5M capital footprint, and
across the 2,413 ZIP-code areas in the sampling frame that is $7.2B-$12.1B of
capital allocation (`outputs/metrics/scope.json`, `capital.label`, run
`20260912-222301-dbde`). Those decisions are made inside proprietary models. The
public bears their consequences — property values, air quality, municipal
budgets, and tax abatements granted to attract facilities — with no way to check
the reasoning. Siting Atlas is an open, reproducible model of that decision,
built entirely from public data.

**This proposal is unusual in one respect and the difference is deliberate. The
central model has already been specified, built, fitted and found to fail, and
that failure is the document's principal finding.** A complementary log-log
discrete-time hazard model of service enablement was fitted on a panel of 43
dated delivery stations. On the held-out split it scores a Brier of 0.019522
against a constant predictor's 0.019614 over the same 8,044 rows, and a
calibration error of 0.00863 against the constant's 0.00005 — that is, a model
that ignores every covariate and predicts the same number everywhere is better
calibrated than ours by a factor of 170. One of three covariates was
distinguishable from zero: households, at a hazard ratio of 1.00005, which
translates as *"Amazon builds where the people are"*. All figures are read from
`experiments/hazard-model/artefacts/hazard_report.json`, run
`20260914-002509-2374`. That specification was later revived on 6.7 times the
events and still lost every calibration comparison it was given (§5.3).

The diagnosis is the unit of analysis. One delivery station switches on a median
of 39 ZIP-code areas at once by drawing a fifteen-mile catchment — mean 52.4,
maximum 317 — so the panel's ZCTA-level events are not independent
observations; the artefact's own power block bounds the real decision count at
28 metro-quarter episodes to 38 usable facilities. The likelihood being
maximised assumes "that each decision maker's choice is independent of that of
other decision makers" (Train 2009, §3.7.1,
p. 61), and a fifteen-mile disk destroys exactly that. The model spent its
capacity learning to draw circles.

The successor is a conditional ZIP-choice model — *given that a station opens in
metro `m` in period `tau`, which ZCTA does it go in* — specified in full in
`docs/MODEL_SPEC.md` and reproduced in §5.4. **It has now been fitted**, on the
104-row national frame rather than the ten-metro pilot, and the result is the
second negative finding in this document.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The last-mile paradigm shift

National fulfilment centres that once shipped to any US address in two to five
days are being complemented — and in dense metros replaced — by delivery
stations, same-day distribution centres and oversize nodes positioned within
thirty minutes of the average consumer. Amazon operated 175 US same-day-capable
facilities in 2024 and committed to 200 or more by the end of 2026 (Amazon 10-K,
2024). In June 2025 it announced over $4 billion to extend same-day and next-day
service to more than 4,000 smaller cities, towns and rural communities by the end
of 2026. Walmart, Target and Costco have announced parallel expansions.

The consumer-facing consequence is a delivery-speed race. The operator-facing
consequence is a capital-allocation problem of unusual granularity: a wrong
answer at ZIP-code level converts a multi-million-dollar investment into a
standing operating loss. The public-facing consequence is that private siting
decisions now reshape neighbourhoods faster than any public process can review
them.

### 1.2 The analytical problem

Adding a ZIP Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA) to a same-day footprint is not a
demand-forecasting problem. It is a joint problem across three interacting
quantities.

- **Incremental demand.** What fraction of post-launch orders are genuinely new
  spend, and what fraction shifted tier from existing standard orders?
- **Route-density economics.** Cost per delivered package falls with drop density
  (Daganzo, 1984). An area with weak density can be unprofitable even at high
  average order value.
- **Spillovers between activations.** Launching in area A changes behaviour and
  cost in nearby areas B and C, so the net present values of individual areas do
  not add.

There is a fourth quantity, and it is the one this project spent the most effort
on: **the decision itself is not observed at the grain at which its consequences
are observed.** The operator signs a lease on one building; coverage of dozens of
ZIP-code areas follows mechanically from a van catchment. A model whose rows are
ZIP-code areas is therefore modelling the consequence and calling it the
decision. That distinction is the subject of §5.3, and getting it wrong is the
largest single error the project made.

### 1.3 Why this matters now, and to whom

Three forces make the problem timely. Unstructured competitor intelligence —
press releases, permit filings, enforcement records — can now be integrated into
quantitative pipelines at near zero marginal cost. Spatial analysis that once
needed a cluster runs on a laptop. And, least discussed, the regulatory
environment has changed.

The South Coast Air Quality Management District's Rule 2305 — the Warehouse
Indirect Source Rule, adopted 2021 and approved by the US EPA — obliges
warehouses at or above 100,000 square feet to earn emission-reduction points or
pay mitigation fees, and other regions are considering equivalents. Air districts
operating such rules need to anticipate where warehouses will appear in order to
plan mitigation capacity. Municipalities granting tax abatements to attract
facilities need the counterfactual: would the operator have built here anyway?
Neither has an open model available.

### 1.4 Research questions

The questions are stated so that a negative answer is a result rather than a
failure. All four now have at least a partial answer, and three of those answers
are negative.

**RQ1. At what unit of analysis can publicly observable data identify a
last-mile operator's siting decisions, and what precision does the available
sample support?** *Answered at three units, negatively at all three.* At the
ZCTA-quarter unit the answer is that it cannot: §5.3 gives the measurement, and
§5.3 also reports that the same specification, revived on 6.7 times the events
with real opening dates, lost every one of 17 calibration comparisons to a
constant. At the station-siting unit the specification is valid but the sample
supports 9.3 to 12.7 decisions per parameter against a conventional floor of
ten (§3.1), and on measurement the fitted model is one covariate. At the
metro-year unit — the one grain where the coarse public series ought to have
had room to work — the answer was pre-registered before it was tested and came
back **H0**: 0 of 7 held-out years against a baseline that ranks metros by
household count (§5.11). **The question is therefore closed in the negative at
every grain this project could reach**, and closing it that way is the result.

**RQ2. What share of the operator's facility footprint is visible in the public
record at all?** *Answered as a floor, and separately estimated.* A second
independent list of sites permits a capture-recapture estimate of the coverage
of our primary source, and a third list permits a direct count (§3.3): of the
488 US cities in which the industry census lists a delivery station, OSHA holds
a record in 138 — 28.3% — and none at all in the other 350. That is a count
rather than a model, and it is a floor rather than an estimate, because the
list it is counted against is itself incomplete. This question did not appear
in v4 and is the clearest gap in the literature the project has found.

**RQ3. Can validation gates be designed that detect when an agent-proposed write
to an analytical warehouse preserves data integrity while invalidating a
downstream identification assumption?** *Open; the gates are built and run against
the real warehouse, the adversarial evaluation is not.*

**RQ4. Does a formal audit against a published data-quality taxonomy change a
published number, relative to fixing defects as they are noticed?** *Answered,
affirmatively.* §4.5 reports the measurements and names the three defects that
move a result.

The question v4 asked — whether a hazard model can reach an out-of-time AUC of
0.80 or better — has been withdrawn. It was answered in the negative before it
could be asked properly, and it was the wrong question at the wrong unit.

### 2.1 Discrete choice and facility siting

Train (2009), *Discrete Choice Methods with Simulation*, is the reference for the
successor specification and was read in full. Three results carry the design.
§2.2 (p. 11) states the three properties a choice set must have — mutually
exclusive, exhaustive, finite — and, in the sentence our own earlier documents
omitted, that the first two "are not restrictive" because alternatives can nearly
always be redefined to satisfy them (p. 12). §3.4, Example 2 (p. 54) shows that a
destination-choice model over arbitrary zones is invariant to how the zones were
drawn only if representative utility is specified "with parameters inside a log
operation", because attraction variables add across a merger. And §3.7.1 (p. 61)
builds the likelihood on the assumption "that each decision maker's choice is
independent of that of other decision makers".

**Our delta.** None on method; we adopt a conditional logit as specified. The
contribution is the application and, more usefully, the demonstration in §5.3 of
what happens when the independence assumption is violated by geometry rather than
by sampling. The failure is measured rather than argued.

### 2.2 Retail expansion, cannibalisation and economies of density

The two closest published works are both in *Econometrica* and both were read.
Holmes (2011) estimates a lower bound on Wal-Mart's economies of density from
revealed preference, by perturbing observed store-opening dates. Houde, Newberry
and Seim (2023) do the corresponding exercise for Amazon's fulfilment-centre
network, exploiting state nexus tax variation.

Three things follow that this project must state rather than hide.

First, **we are not first, and the honest answer is better than a claim of
novelty.** Both papers used the estimator we would otherwise have reached for.

Second, **their facility tier is not ours.** The phrase "delivery station"
returns zero hits in Houde, Newberry and Seim; they study fulfilment and
sortation centres and explicitly drop specialised sites. Their catchment figures
— destination ZIPs within 150 miles of a sortation centre, which must itself sit
within 25 miles of a fulfilment centre (§2.2, p. 153) — describe sortation, not
last-mile delivery, and do not bear on a fifteen-mile van radius.

Third, **their data is not reproducible by a third party.** Holmes bought store
data from a commercial panel; Houde, Newberry and Seim obtained the network from
the supply-chain consultancy MWPVL. **We did not buy that panel and we did not
go without the data either.** The same consultancy publishes a free public
article — a different document, at a different vintage — whose facility tables
are images rather than text. Those images were read by OCR, which recovered
1,904 facilities and 1,420 opening dates at no cost and with no licence. The
distinction between *the commercial panel* and *the free public page* is the
whole of the project's position and §4.3 sets it out with the evidence.

quantity we measure worst. Holmes §7 assumes opening dates "are all assumed to be
measured without error", and §8.3 states that the procedure yields inconsistent
estimates of the identified set under measurement error in the `x` variables.
Our dates are OSHA inspection dates with externally verified lags of 4, 13, 57,
69 and 345 months. The estimator protects the side we have clean and assumes
clean the side we do not.

**Our delta.** A different facility tier, a free and reproducible data pipeline,
a different question — who gets served, and whether a city can check it — and a
runnable artefact. We claim no methodological novelty here.

### 2.3 Routing and density economics

The continuous-approximation approach to vehicle routing originates with
Beardwood, Halton and Hammersley (1959). Daganzo (1984) derived that per-stop
distance in a uniform-density zone scales as the inverse square root of delivery
density, and supplies the zone-geometry constants the cost model uses.

**Our delta.** None on the mathematics. The engineering contribution is negative
and deliberate: the deployed system carries no routing dependency at all (§5.9).
One finding from a parameter sweep is worth reporting against this literature
rather than for it — the Daganzo constant is the single *least* influential
number in our cost model, moving the median cost per parcel by under one per cent
across its plausible range, while the operational quantities that dominate the
answer (service time per stop, parcels per stop, van lease, labour hours) appear
in no academic paper at all. A reviewer attacking Daganzo is attacking the
strongest wall in the building.

### 2.4 Facility location as an optimisation problem

Klose and Drexl (2005) survey facility-location models for distribution-system
design and were read in full. Their taxonomy places our problem precisely: a
minsum objective (§2, point 2), a network rather than a continuous topology, no
fixed cost per depot to trade against distance, and a throughput capacity we
choose not to enforce per depot. That combination is the **p-median problem**,
their equations (1a)-(1e), with the number of depots fixed by the *aggregate*
capacity constraint (6) rather than the per-depot constraint (11) — a relaxation
they themselves identify as the standard one, since it is the basis of the
aggregate-capacity plant location problem which has "a dominant role as a
relaxation when solving" the capacitated models (§5.1, p. 7 of the proof copy).

Hakimi (1964), also read in full, is where the licence to choose depots from a
finite candidate list comes from. **We are careful about how far it reaches, and
the careful version is weaker than the one usually cited.** The 1964 paper proves
that an absolute median of a weighted graph is always at a vertex — for *one*
facility, on a *graph*. There is no `p` anywhere in it; the p-facility result is
Hakimi (1965), which this project has not obtained. We place 334 depots and we
measure great-circle distance in the plane, where the corresponding object is the
Weber point and is in general not at a demand point. So the node restriction is a
genuine restriction here and not a free lunch, and §5.5 defends it with a
measurement instead of a theorem.

**Our delta.** None. This is a correction to our own earlier work, not a
contribution: the depot layer previously used k-means, which minimises squared
distance, while the cost function it feeds is linear in distance.

### 2.5 Forecast evaluation and proper scoring rules

Gneiting and Raftery (2007), *JASA* 102(477), was read in full, and it supplies
the grounds on which this project's headline metric can be defended *as a
metric*, independently of what it happened to say about our model. A scoring rule
is proper if quoting your true belief maximises the expected score, and strictly
proper if that maximum is unique (§2.1, p. 360). The Brier score is strictly
proper (§3.1, p. 363). Skill scores of the form `1 - S_model/S_ref` are not: they
"are generally improper, even if the underlying scoring rule `S` is proper"
(§2.3, p. 362), with Murphy (1973) proving only asymptotic propriety and Mason
(2004)'s propriety claim named there and called generally incorrect.

The paper's argument for why this matters is a case study about the weather, not
about us, which is precisely its value. On 16,015 real sea-level-pressure
forecasts, four proper scores recovered a variance-inflation factor between 1.62
and 2.41 against a truth near 1.55; two improper but intuitively appealing
scores, one of them in published operational use, returned 0.05 and 0.02 — that
is, they chose a model claiming essentially no uncertainty. The authors conclude
that "their use may result in misguided scientific inferences, as in this
experiment" (§8.2, p. 374). That rule was published in March 2007. It is not one
we invented in September 2026 after looking at a disappointing AUC.

**One thing must be stated precisely, because the overclaim is tempting.**
Gneiting and Raftery do not criticise, condemn or discuss AUC. A full-text search
of all twenty pages returns zero occurrences of AUC, ROC, receiver, area under,
or discrimination; the only adjacent citation is a pointer to Pepe (2003) as
future work in a neighbouring field. The correct and sufficient argument is
structural: a scoring rule is evaluated on a single forecast-observation pair and
averaged over cases (§2.1 p. 360, §2.3 p. 362), whereas AUC is a rank statistic
over *pairs* of cases and cannot be written in that form. **Propriety is
therefore undefined for AUC rather than violated by it.** That is weaker than the
claim a reader might expect and it is enough, because what a headline metric must
supply is exactly the guarantee AUC cannot.

**Our delta.** None. We adopt the apparatus. What is worth recording is that the
metric change was justified from a source read before the result was interpreted,
and that the adjacent, stronger claim was checked and rejected.

### 2.6 Data quality and statistical data editing

This stream did not appear in v4 at all, and its absence was a defect rather than
a scope choice. Six works were obtained and read in full.

Rahm and Do (2000) give the canonical taxonomy of data-quality problems. It is
commonly summarised as a two-by-two of single-source versus multi-source and
schema versus instance level; it is not. There is a third axis of **scope** —
attribute, record, record type, source (§2.1) — and it is the axis that says
which kind of check can find a class at all. Duplicated and contradicting records
are record-type scope, so no amount of per-attribute profiling will ever surface
them. That is exactly why our own coverage report shows the facility data as
clean while three records in it contradict each other. Their §3 also names a
fifth process phase, backflow, that we had never named.

Fellegi and Holt (1976) is the founding paper of automatic edit and imputation.
Its three criteria — minimum change, imputation rules derived from edit rules,
and preservation of the frequency distribution — are what condemn an ad-hoc
tie-break, and its §1 option 5 demolishes complete-case deletion in the year
before MCAR had a name: deleting records that fail edits "would involve an
implicit assumption that the statistical inferences are unaffected by such
deletions. This is equivalent to assuming that the deleted records have the same
distribution as the satisfactory records."

Winkler (1999, RR99-01) reports how those methods are actually implemented in
statistical agencies and, usefully, contradicts a cost estimate this project had
been carrying. It also supplies the reframing that resolves our outlier problem:
selective or macro editing ranks records by their influence on a published total
rather than by their distance from a median.

Van den Broeck and colleagues (2005) give the procedure — a *repeating* cycle of
screening, diagnosis and treatment, with four screening oddities, four possible
diagnoses per data point and exactly three treatments — and the name for the
defect class that had been costing us most: an **erroneous inlier**, "data points
generated by error but falling within the expected range", of which they predict
that they "will often escape detection".

Two surveys complete the set. Mohammed and colleagues (2024) give the current
term of art for that same class, *hidden missing values*, and the observation
that deletions and imputations are themselves metadata. Ehrlinger, Rusz and Woess
(2019) evaluated thirteen production data-quality tools against a 43-item
requirements catalogue and found that zero implement a consistency metric, zero a
timeliness metric, and one an accuracy metric that requires a gold standard.

**Our delta.** The contribution is not to the literature; it is that a working
analytical pipeline has been audited against this taxonomy and the audit is
published with its measurements, including the ones that are unflattering. §4.5
reports it. The audit changed three published positions, and one of the three was
our own classification of our own worst defect.

### 2.7 LLM agents that write to analytical systems

Retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020) and the ReAct pattern (Yao et
al., 2023) established the interleaving of reasoning and tool use; the Model
Context Protocol formalised a portable tool interface. Applications in analytics
have converged on text-to-SQL agents that are almost universally read-only.

Gated writes are not unexplored, and v4 correctly withdrew a claim to the
contrary after a prior-art check turned up a safety-specification survey, work on
privacy-preserving natural-language database interfaces, text-to-SQL
vulnerability analysis and shipped open-source write gates. We claim no novelty
in gating an agent write.

**Our delta.** That work protects data integrity: do not corrupt rows, do not
leak records, do not drop tables. Our write target is a parameter of an
analytical estimator, which creates a failure class those gates do not cover — a
write that is schema-valid, correctly geocoded, high-confidence and fully logged,
and that nonetheless invalidates a downstream assumption. We name this
*inferential integrity* and propose two gates that detect it (§5.7). The claim is
narrowed relative to v4 and is stated so that a product datasheet cannot falsify
it.

Commercial practice must be acknowledged with the same care as the literature. A
capable market already sells siting analytics; the question for a research
contribution is not whether the tools exist but what none of them does.

a two-list intersection is not capture-recapture, and MWPVL is itself
incomplete and says so about this very table. The modelled estimate is the
Chapman and Chao pair above; the 28.3% is the thing that was counted. They are
different quantities and are not averaged.

**The direction of the bias is not provisional, and it is the interesting part.**
Lincoln-Petersen assumes the two lists are independent. Ours are not: a site with
more workers, longer operation and more grievance is more likely to appear in
both an enforcement record and a labour filing. Positive dependence between the
lists *deflates* the estimated total, which means any Lincoln-Petersen figure is
an **upper bound** on coverage, not a point estimate. Saying so is what
distinguishes a measurement from a reassurance. The refinement being built
replaces the two-list estimator with Chao's lower-bound estimator and a
log-linear model over three or four lists, which is the standard way to admit
dependence.

## 4. Data

### 4.1 Sources

Fourteen public sources are registered with their licence, update cadence and
reporting lag, and are ingested through a common extract-load-transform pipeline
into twelve typed parquet tables and a DuckDB star schema. No licensed panel and
no paid API is required, which is what makes the system reproducible by a third
party. Sources are selected not only for what they measure but for how often they
update: two are included specifically to offset the reporting lag in the census
series (§4.2).

The full per-source documentation — endpoint, licence, measured reachability,
manual acquisition steps where the host cannot fetch automatically — is generated
from the source registry into `docs/data/`, one Markdown file per source, and is
not restated here.

### 4.2 Data currency and the reporting lag

American Community Survey five-year estimates carry an 18 to 24 month reporting
lag. Taken alone this would be a serious weakness in a model of decisions being
made now. Three features of the design address it, and one residual risk is
stated rather than resolved.

First, and most importantly, **the lag is shared with the decision-maker.**
Operators plan siting from the same public census releases; there is no private
real-time census. Our disadvantage relative to an operator lies in order history,
not in demographic covariates — which is precisely why the estimand in §4.3 is
the siting decision rather than demand.

Second, **siting decisions have long lead times.** Site search, lease
negotiation, permitting, fit-out and hiring together span roughly 18 to 36
months, so a facility opening in 2025 was decided in 2023 on 2021-22 covariates.
Aligning covariate vintage to decision vintage is therefore correct
specification, not a compromise. Using contemporaneous data to explain a decision
taken two years earlier would give the model information the decision-maker did
not have.

Third, **the covariates that genuinely move quickly are sourced quickly.** Zillow
rent and value indices are monthly; the Census Building Permits Survey is monthly
and is a forward indicator, since construction precedes population; energy prices
are monthly. Slow sources supply level features and fast sources supply
rate-of-change features, so the model observes trajectory rather than only
position.

**Figure 6.** Sources ordered by reporting lag, against the operator's own
decision lead time. The lags are transcribed from each source's own documentation
rather than measured by the ingest layer, so they can silently go out of date;
the measured column in `docs/data/README.md` is the authority.
(figure: docs/figures/fig13_currency.png)

The residual risk is stated rather than resolved. An area that changed sharply
within the last eighteen months and generates no permit or rent signal will be
misclassified. Where fast and slow signals disagree materially, the area is
flagged as low-confidence in the application rather than silently ranked.

There is also a **measured defect** in the current implementation, and §4.5
carries it as finding F8: every panel row labelled 2018Q1 asserts May-2025 metro
wages, 2024 environmental indicators, 2019-2023 pooled demographics, 2022
business counts and 2020 boundaries as contemporaneous facts. The spread between
the earliest and latest vintage inside a single row is seven years, and nothing
in the data records the vintage. Twenty-six of 35 numeric columns are constant
across all 32 quarters. The design decision to pin vintages is defensible; the
absence of any trace of it in the data is not.

is that the panel now exists and its provenance can be described honestly.

The fabrication is the finding worth carrying. It was caught only because a

**What makes the source usable is also what makes it weak.** OSHA answers "when
can we prove this site was operating", not "when did it open". That converts a
missing-data problem into a **censoring** problem, which survival analysis exists
to handle — but only if the censoring is modelled, which it is not yet (§6). The
looseness has been measured rather than asserted: on the five addresses that also
appear in a 2012 industry table with real opening months, the "operating by"
bound held five times out of five, and the lags to first inspection were 4, 13,
57, 69 and 345 months. One site opened in 1997 and was first inspected in 2026.
Five addresses is not a measurement, and §3.3 is the attempt to get a real one.

**A sixth route was tried after the fifth worked, and it failed: dating
buildings from satellite imagery.** The logic was sound and the failure is
worth more than a silent omission. OSHA gives an upper bound on an opening
date. Sentinel-2 photographs every point on Earth every five days, free, back
to 2015, and when a delivery station is built, bare ground becomes a large
bright roof and a car park. An upper bound from OSHA plus a lower bound from
imagery is an *interval*, which is something the statistics can use, and it is
the single thing that would unblock the timing factor of §5.4's decomposition.
The script is `data/collection/satellite/colab_date_from_satellite.py` and it
ran in Google Colab on 2026-09-14.

An estimate that dates construction after the building was observed operating

the method emits does not discriminate between the impossible and the
plausible, so there is no filter that rescues the good ones. The cause is the
changepoint detector rather than the imagery: the scenes are there and the
detector picks the wrong break in them. **The 68 surviving estimates are not
presented as dates anywhere in this project and must not be**, because the
only reason they survive is that they happen to fall on the right side of a
test that a third of their siblings fail. The result is reported here as a
negative one. The source table sits outside the repository, at
`../satellite_dates.csv`, and getting it under version control with the rest
is an outstanding item.

### 4.4 Sampling frame

The pilot frame is ten metros comprising **2,413 ZCTAs** under the OMB 2023 CBSA
delineation, spanning 84 counties: the San Francisco Bay Area, New York, Chicago,
Austin, Seattle, Denver, Miami, Nashville, Phoenix and Boise. All counts are read
from `outputs/metrics/scope.json` (run `20260912-222301-dbde`), which exists so
that no headline figure is ever typed into prose; an earlier draft of this
proposal claimed approximately 5,200 ZCTAs, a figure that had been typed once and
copied.

Metros are identified by five-digit CBSA code and never by title. Six of the ten
have a different title in the 2023 delineation than in the 2020 one, so a
title-based join would have silently dropped six metros on a routine reference
refresh, and the symptom would have been "fewer rows" rather than an error.

Phoenix and Boise were withheld from training for out-of-metro validation. That
device did not survive contact with the data and the document says so: the two
metros hold **two dated delivery stations between them**, so the held-out check
is a smoke test for gross failure and not a test of geographic transfer. The
artefact records this in its own caveat field and this proposal does not quote
the held-out figure as evidence about generalisation.

The panel itself is a dense cross join of 33,791 national ZCTAs with 32 quarters
— 1,081,312 rows, 14.6 MB. Density is deliberate: filtering to observed rows
would hand the model a sample-selection problem, because the sources with the
best coverage are the urban ones. The row count is not a claim to scale, and
§5.9 says so.

### 4.5 The data-quality audit

Every class in Rahm and Do's Tables 1 and 2 was scored against the pipeline, on
all three of their axes — single-source versus multi-source, schema versus
instance level, and the scope axis (attribute, record, record type, source) that
most summaries drop. The full scorecard, with a measured number against every
row, is `docs/data/DATA_QUALITY.md`. The shape of the result is worth stating in
one line: **screening is decent, diagnosis is patchy, treatment is almost absent,
and the audit trail that would make treatment reversible does not exist.** The
project is good at noticing and bad at acting, which is a better failure than the
reverse and is not "best in class".

**The three findings that change a published number.**

```
  F2  CENSORING FLAGS COMPUTED, THEN DROPPED
      86 ZCTAs carry a median household income of exactly $250,001 and 83 a
      median home value of exactly $2,000,001.  Those are ACS top-codes --
      bounds, not measurements -- and 36 and 46 of them respectively reach
      the published cost ranking, where income drives parcel demand through
      an income elasticity.  Two bottom-codes ($2,499 income on 23 ZCTAs,
      $9,999 home value on 20) had never been looked for at all.

  F4  THREE RECORDS THAT CONTRADICT EACH OTHER ABOUT THE OUTCOME
      The national facility file's 104 rows describe 101 buildings.  The
      three pairs are not duplicates: our own matcher pairs all three at
      score 1.000 with zero false positives, so identity is not in doubt.
      What they disagree about is the OPENING DATE, by 10, 6 and 3 quarters.
      The loader resolved this by taking the earlier date, silently and
      always in the same direction.  At a median 39 ZIPs per station that is
      on the order of 120 ZCTA cells of the target variable whose enablement
      quarter is set by an unadjudicated conflict -- and min() is directional,
      so it is bias rather than noise.  (This block read 58 ZIPs and 170
      cells until 2026-09-15; the catchment median is measured at 39 and the
      120 is 3 x 39 arithmetic, not an artefact field.)

  F5  AN IMPUTATION DEFAULT VISIBLE IN THE OUTCOME
      19 of 43 facilities (44.2%) have no recorded opening quarter and were
      defaulted to Q1.  The consequence is measurable and was in fact
      measured: 35.47% of fitted events fall in Q1 against 25% expected
      under a uniform quarter.  The project measured that artefact, wrote it
      to a metrics file, and did nothing with it.
```

Reading the literature merged two of those. F2 and F5 are **one defect, not
two**: a dummy value occupying a field that should say "unknown", invisible to
every range check because the value is plausible. Rahm and Do's Table 2 calls the
class *missing values* and its canonical example is a sentinel telephone number;
Van den Broeck and colleagues name the mechanism an **erroneous inlier** and
correctly predict that such values "will often escape detection"; the current
term of art is a **hidden missing value**. One declared per-source placeholder
registry closes both, rather than two unrelated remediations. That
reclassification is the single most useful thing the reading produced, and it was
possible only because the papers were opened rather than recalled — an earlier
version of our own audit had filed the top-code under the wrong class.

**What has since been built, and it is a partial fix rather than a repair.** A
declared sentinel registry now exists in the source tree and the censoring
indicators reach the panel: `outputs/metrics/panel_report_expanded.json`
(run `20260915-195503-5761`, the panel rebuilt on the expanded facility frame)
reports 50 columns and lists six flags under `quality_flags.carried` —
`median_household_income_topcoded`, `median_home_value_topcoded`,
`median_household_income_bottomcoded`, `median_home_value_bottomcoded`,
`open_quarter_imputed` and `wage_suppressed`. Two corrections to what this
paragraph used to say: the **bottom**-codes that F2 records as never having
been looked for are now carried as well, and `rent_observed` is **not** in that
list — it is emitted by the panel build but is not registered as a quality
flag, and naming it here as one was wrong. The three contradicting records
now raise rather than resolving themselves silently. **But no model or cost
consumer conditions on any of those flags yet, and the unknown opening quarters
are still unknown.** The flags make the problem visible; they do not make it go
away, and §5.4 is where that distinction becomes a decision.

**Three further findings that do not move a number today but constrain what may
be claimed.**

*Outliers are unscreened and mostly real.* A robust screen flags 28.36% of ZCTAs
on household density, and the extremes are Manhattan: ZCTA 10069 is 6,669 people
on 0.041 square miles, which is arithmetically and substantively correct. A
three-sigma filter would delete the answer. The literature points away from the
screen we were about to build — Rahm and Do prescribe a *declared plausibility
band* per column rather than a distributional test, Winkler reframes the task as
ranking by influence on the published total rather than by distance from the
median, and Van den Broeck supplies four verdicts per flagged point (erroneous,
true extreme, true normal, idiopathic) so that the output is a diagnosis rather
than a flag. The redesigned screen is specified in `docs/data/DATA_QUALITY.md`
R9 and is not built.

*Three joins present a coarse geography under a fine column name.* Five
environmental columns are county means wearing ZCTA column names, at about 10.5
ZCTAs per distinct value; the metro wage columns are worse, at 52.9. A model
regressing on those columns has standard errors wrong by roughly the square root
of that factor. The code knows this and says so in a comment; nothing in the data
records it.

*One join loses 58% of its keys.* The metro wage series matches 388 of 928 CBSAs,
and the 540 it omits are the small ones, so the wage covariates are missing
exactly where missingness correlates with size — the same shape as the rent
problem below, at 14x instead of 63.6x. None of these unmatched rates is computed
by the pipeline; all eighteen were computed for the audit, in about forty lines.

**What the audit found that is good, stated because an audit that only finds
fault is not an audit.** The grain contract is real and holds exactly. ZIP-code
zero-padding is intact at every entry point, which is the most common silent
data-loss bug in ZIP-level work. The record linkage implements a published
three-way decision rule, measures its thresholds off a real sweep rather than
borrowing them, and *declines* to implement a component of the method for reasons
found in the source — declining for a sourced reason is a higher standard than
implementing. Sources whose grain cannot be inferred are refused rather than
guessed at. And the rent availability indicator is textbook: rather than imputing
an 89%-missing column or dropping the rows, the panel emits an explicit flag with
a comment naming the exact failure it prevents.

### 4.6 Missingness, and why we do not impute

The largest missingness in the dataset is the rent index, and it is not random.

```
  ZCTAs with at least one missing quarter     31,864 of 33,791    94.30%
  ZCTAs missing in all 32 quarters            27,199 of 33,791    80.49%
  ZCTAs absent from the source file entirely  25,247 of 33,791    74.72%

  comparing the observed and missing strata   observed   missing   ratio
  median household density, per sq mi          1,555.6      24.5   63.6x
  median population density, per sq mi         3,920.4      66.5   59.0x
  median households                             16,166       869   18.6x
```

Density is the variable the entire cost argument turns on. Any analysis
conditioned on the rent index being present is therefore fitted on a sample whose
median area is **63.6 times denser** than the population it is meant to describe,
and its standard errors understate the extrapolation risk to the other 94%.

**What may and may not be concluded from that, stated carefully.** What the
measurement establishes is that the missingness is **not MCAR**. Whether it is
MAR or MNAR is not testable from the data, because it turns on whether
missingness depends on the unobserved rent level after conditioning on what we do
observe, and the unobserved rent is by construction unobserved. The publication
threshold plausibly depends on listing volume, which we observe, and plausibly
also on the rent level itself, which we do not. So MAR is an *argument* we would
be making, not a finding — and our own earlier documentation overstated this,
which is corrected in `docs/research/NOTES_little_rubin_ch1_3.md` §8. The honest
position is: measured not-MCAR, MNAR not excluded, and that is what licenses
bounds rather than imputation.

**The sharpest citation against complete-case analysis turns out to be from
1976.** Fellegi and Holt enumerate five options when a record fails an edit, and
option five is deletion. Their verdict, written before the missing-data
vocabulary existed: deletion "would involve an implicit assumption that the
statistical inferences are unaffected by such deletions. This is equivalent to
assuming that the deleted records have the same distribution as the satisfactory
records." Here we can *show* that assumption failing.

**We nevertheless decline to impute the rent index, and the reason is not
squeamishness.** Imputing 94% of a column from the 6% that is a systematically
different kind of place produces a column that looks like data and is a
regression prediction. The column is not used by the cost model and is not in the
successor specification's covariate list at all, for an unrelated and stronger
reason given in §5.4. What the literature does ask for here, and what we commit
to, is cheaper and more decisive than an imputation: **report the outcome both
ways** — with and without the dropped stratum — and publish the difference. That
check has never been run and it is on the critical path.

Every drop and imputation in the pipeline is enumerated with a measured count in
`docs/data/DATA_QUALITY.md` F9. Two bind. The cost model drops 80 of 2,413 pilot
ZCTAs on a households filter, of which 55 have zero population — arguably an
impossible value, for which deletion is the prescribed treatment — and 23 have
people and no households, which is not impossible but group quarters, and
deleting those is not covered by any rule we can cite. The model's panel source
drops 131 of 2,206 in-scope units for an incomplete covariate; the event-rate
distortion is mild (51.1% of dropped units were ever enabled against 54.5% of
kept) but the covariate support is cut, so the model never sees an area below
about 500 people. That is defensible as a scope restriction and indefensible as
an undeclared one, and it is declared here.

### 4.7 Limitations that admit no mitigation

Four limitations have no mitigation and are therefore not mitigated. Stating that
plainly is what makes the mitigated ones credible.

**Order volume, revenue and margin are unobservable in public data.** No accuracy
claim is made on dollar figures, and results are reported in units of
contribution margin with explicit break-even thresholds, so that no unobservable
revenue figure is ever asserted (§5.6).

**No public number exists against which a cannibalisation estimate could be
validated.** If that estimand is attempted, the deliverable is a placebo
distribution and confidence bands under stated assumptions, not a claim of
correctness.

**Internal constraints are invisible.** Existing lease obligations, capital
rationing and executive preference do not appear in any public source, which is
why model output is framed as operator-consistent desirability rather than
objective viability.

**Public data may have an accuracy ceiling below what the decision requires.**
That possibility is RQ1 and RQ2, and the answer so far is partly negative. It is
reported as a finding rather than suppressed, which is the whole design of this
document.

A fifth belongs with them now and did not appear in v4. **The sample is a
convenience sample discovered through an enforcement process.** The 43 stations
were found by searching inspection records for buildings that exist, so we
observe chosen alternatives *because* they were chosen. Train §3.7.2 covers
choice-based sampling and gives a result under which estimating as if exogenous
is consistent for everything except alternative-specific constants — which we do
not have — but that result assumes a purely choice-based sample with a known
population share. Ours has an unknown and non-random discovery process. This is
flagged as an unquantified threat and is not addressed. Objective 2 (§3.3) is the
attempt to put a number on part of it.

**A sixth changes character rather than disappearing, and it is new on
2026-09-14.** 589 of the 693 rows now in the facility frame (§4.8) were not
discovered through an enforcement process at all; they were published by a
consultancy tracking announcements, permits and trade press. The selection
threat is not removed. It is *replaced* by a partially independent one — a
commercial tracker's attention, which skews toward large and newsworthy
buildings — and two non-identical selection mechanisms are a better position
than one, because their disagreement is measurable where a single mechanism's
bias is not. The paragraph above remains correct about the 104 hand-verified
rows the fitted model uses.

### 4.8 The opening dates, recovered

*This section is new on 2026-09-14 and is placed at the end of §4 so that
nothing above it needed renumbering. It reports the removal of the constraint
that §4.3, §4.7, §5.4 and §6 defects E, M and O are all written from inside.*

Every date in this project has been an **upper bound**. OSHA proves a building
was operating by the day an inspector stood in it and says nothing about when it
opened; the panel could assert "open by 2019" and never "opened in 2019". That
single gap blocked the lag guard on the industry covariate (§6 defect M), blocked
any use of time in the choice model, and made before-and-after designs
impossible. Two routes had been tried and failed (§4.3). A third worked.

#### 4.8.1 What was read, and why plain OCR was abandoned

MWPVL International's free public article documenting Amazon's distribution
network carries its facility tables as **images, not text** — thirteen table
images totalling 152,915 pixel-rows, with no machine-readable content.

The technically material decision is a negative one. Plain OCR was implemented,
measured and **abandoned**. Table rows wrap over two to four printed lines, and
ordinary reading order splits a date across them — the month on one line and the
year on the next — with no reliable rule for rejoining them. The pipeline instead
recovers each word *with its pixel coordinates* and reconstructs the table
geometrically: columns from horizontal position, rows from vertical position.

One further decision earned its place, because without it 40% of the document
would have been lost silently rather than loudly. Rows are anchored on a US
five-digit postal code. Canadian, Japanese and UK postcodes are not five digits,
so on the three rest-of-world tables the anchor finds almost nothing and every
facility collapses into a handful of merged rows — **with no error and a
plausible-looking output**, which is this project's documented failure mode. The
fallback anchors on an Amazon facility code (`YYC1`, `MMG1`, `HPX1`) instead, and
the pipeline picks whichever strategy clears a minimum anchor density. Those
three tables are 61,428 of the 152,915 pixel-rows, 40.2%.

## 5. Proposed Methods

### 5.1 System architecture

The system decomposes into a public-source ingest layer, a dimensional warehouse,
a set of analytical models, a decision layer, and two delivery surfaces — one for
operators and one for public planning. Stages are numbered L0 to L5 and are owned
individually, because assigning modules without assigning stages is how a shared
pipeline becomes nobody's responsibility.

```
  L0  acquire      DONE      9 sources automatic, 5 by hand
  L1  normalise    DONE      12 typed parquet tables
  L2  warehouse    DONE      DuckDB star schema, 5 tables
  L3  panel        DONE      1,081,312 rows x 50 columns
  L4  cost         DONE      2,333 ZCTAs over a solved depot network
  L4  optimise     DONE      bundle selection with a reported optimality gap
  L4  siting model DONE AT THREE GRAINS, AND NEGATIVE AT ALL THREE
                     ZCTA-quarter hazard, ZCTA choice, CBSA-year entry
                     the third was pre-registered and hashed first
  L4  agent gates  PARTIAL   six gates run on the real warehouse
  L5  figures      DONE      four chart families x five scenarios
  L5  dashboard    DONE      shares chart code with the figure layer
  L5  operator view NOT STARTED
```

Note the shape of that. **The engineering works and the science does not.** The
one component that needed a valid unit of analysis is the one that was never
given one.

### 5.2 Dimensional warehouse

The warehouse is a Kimball-style star schema in DuckDB. DuckDB is chosen because
columnar vectorised execution suits the queries the application issues, its
spatial extension provides the geometry functions natively, and the entire
warehouse is a single portable file — which is what makes peer-review
reproduction feasible.

**Figure 7.** Star schema. The scenario dimension is what makes auditability real
rather than nominal. (figure: docs/figures/fig02_star_schema.png)

The scenario dimension deserves comment. Every agent-proposed mutation of an
estimator parameter creates a scenario row recording which parameter version,
which mutation and which random seed produced a given result, so any published
number can be replayed exactly. Without this, a system whose parameters change in
response to text is not auditable in any meaningful sense.

One contract in this layer is worth naming because it is the strongest piece of
validation in the repository: the panel build asserts its own grain and fails if
the table is not exactly one row per area and quarter. It holds exactly —
1,081,312 rows, 1,081,312 distinct pairs, 33,791 x 32. Its docstring is unusually
honest about why it exists, observing that the property held previously by luck
rather than by contract, and that this is the contract.

### 5.3 The model that was built, and what it measured

**The specification.** For area `i` in quarter `t`, the hazard of first
enablement was modelled as a complementary log-log discrete-time hazard over
covariates, with a flexible baseline in time, fitted by penalised maximum
likelihood with clustered standard errors and unit-wise splits. It was a
defensible choice *given the unit*: the estimand is whether and when an area is
first switched on, that is a time-to-event question with right-censoring, and a
plain classifier would have to either discard the timing or treat "not enabled by
2025" as "never enabled". The unit was never examined.

**The result.** All figures from `experiments/hazard-model/artefacts/hazard_report.json`, run
`20260914-002509-2374`; regenerate with `make model`.

hazard ratio of 1.00005 — which is the model having learned *"the operator builds
where the people are"*: true, and not worth a model.

**Two reporting rules apply to that table and they are part of the result.**

*The raw Brier pair is the headline, never the skill score.* Both numbers in a
row are over the same rows, which is the comparability condition Gneiting and
Raftery state at §2.3 p. 362, and the same section states that skill scores "are
generally improper, even if the underlying scoring rule `S` is proper". The
artefact still emits a skill number; it is a normalisation for readability and
not the estimand.

*The geographic hold-out is not quoted as a transfer result.* The artefact's own
caveat field records that the two withheld metros hold two dated stations between
them, "so this is a smoke test for gross failure and not a test of geographic
transfer". Earlier versions of several project documents quoted its figure bare,
which overstated the evidence against our own model.

**The diagnosis: the unit of analysis, not the sample size.** The operator does
not switch on ZIP codes. It signs a lease on one building, and coverage follows
mechanically from a fifteen-mile van catchment — **a median of 39 areas at a
time, a mean of 52.4, a maximum of 317**
(`experiments/hazard-model/artefacts/hazard_revival.json`,

artefact's own power block bounds the real count between 28 metro-quarter
episodes and 38 usable facilities against five parameters, and publishes all
three ratios rather than the flattering one, with the note: *"The nominal ratio
counts ZCTAs and is not evidence of power."*

demonstration in the project that **more data does not repair a defect in the
unit of analysis**, and it is worth more than the original retirement was,
because the original retirement could have been sample size and this one cannot.

**The citation for that, corrected.** Several of this project's own documents
attributed the failure to Train §2.2's requirement that a choice set be mutually
exclusive. Having read §2.2 in full, that attribution is wrong and the correction
makes the argument stronger rather than weaker. §2.2 governs a choice set facing
a decision maker, and a hazard on area-quarters has no decision maker choosing
among those rows, so exclusivity is not a property they can have or lack. Worse
for the rhetoric, Train says the criterion is easy to satisfy: "The first and
second criteria are not restrictive. Appropriate definition of alternatives can
nearly always assure that the alternatives are mutually exclusive" (p. 12), and
he gives a two-line repair recipe. **The assumption actually violated is in
Chapter 3**, where the likelihood is built (§3.7.1, p. 61):

> "Assuming that each decision maker's choice is independent of that of other
> decision makers, the probability of each person in the sample choosing the
> alternative that he was observed actually to choose is
> `L(beta) = prod_n prod_i (P_ni)^{y_ni}`."

*Independence across observations.* That is what a fifteen-mile disk destroys.
The standard name for the failure is clustering, or pseudo-replication, not
non-exclusivity. The diagnosis stands; only the citation and the word were wrong.
Train §2.2 is the authority for building the *successor* choice set, and §5.4
uses it that way.

**A useful way to see the whole thing.** If you want to know which houses flood,
you model where the river breaches and then compute which houses sit below the
water line. You do not model five hundred houses as five hundred independent
events, count them as five hundred pieces of evidence, and report spectacular
confidence in a conclusion drawn from one observation.

**One thing that was measured properly and should be reported as such.** v4
promised split conformal prediction with empirically verified coverage, and
described the coverage as verified at 90% before it had been computed. It has now
been computed, and the measured number is not 90%.

### 5.4 The successor specification

The successor is a **conditional ZIP-choice model**. The full specification,
written before any model code and with every citation opened, is
`docs/MODEL_SPEC.md`; this section states it and the four decisions a reader is
most likely to challenge.

**The estimand.** *Conditional on the operator opening one last-mile delivery
station in metro `m` during structural period `tau`, the probability that the
station is sited in area `j` rather than in any other area of metro `m`.*

It is deliberately not three other things. It is not "when does an area get
switched on" — the timing factor has left the estimand and survives only as a
conditioning variable. It is not causal: the coefficients describe an observed
association under a random-utility representation, and Train is explicit that the
representation does not commit us to the behaviour. And it is not a market-share
model, which is what closes off the BLP route.

**The ZIP-level answer is derived, not abandoned:**

```
  P(an area gets same-day service)
    =  P(a station opens in its metro)          from base rates
    x  P(the chosen site is within 15 miles)    from the choice model
```

This specification supplies the second factor only. The first is a base-rate
exercise on dates that are upper bounds and is out of scope here. Train's §2.6
constrains how the second factor may be aggregated: it must be computed by
enumerating alternatives and summing, never by averaging covariates within a
metro and evaluating once, because the model is nonlinear in its inputs and the
error has a known direction at low probabilities.

**The choice set, demonstrated rather than asserted** — this is the check that
was skipped last time.

```
  mutually exclusive   a station is one building at one address; addresses lie
                       in exactly one area because areas tessellate.  Siting in
                       j necessarily means not siting in k.  A fact about
                       geometry, not a modelling convenience.
  exhaustive           conditionally.  The station must be somewhere in the
                       metro.  Train p.13 licenses this move explicitly and
                       states its price: we give up the ability to model
                       WHETHER a station opens, and must supply that factor
                       separately.  Which is the factorisation above.
  finite               2,333 areas in the pilot frame.  Counted.  The fit
                       reported below is on the national frame instead, where
                       the 38 held-out decisions face 3,998 alternatives in
                       total, a mean of 105 per choice set.
```

**The utility function, and the restriction nobody had stated.** Representative
utility is `V_nj = ln(beta' a_j)`, with the parameters inside a log operation.
This is not a style choice: with zonal alternatives the model is invariant to how
the zones were drawn only if merging two zones adds their attraction variables,
and Train derives the log form from exactly that requirement (§3.4 Example 2,
p. 54). Areas are Census constructs built from postal delivery routes, so
invariance to their boundaries is worth paying for.

The consequence is a real restriction and it costs us our most interesting
covariate. **Additive means extensive.** Counts add across a merger; medians,
rates, ratios and densities do not. So the attraction vector is households, land
area, establishments and — added after the first fit — the count of NAICS 493
warehousing and storage establishments, all four extensive. Median household
income, the one covariate with an economic story attached, is **excluded on
principle**, because the median of a merged zone is not the sum of two medians.

> **A claimed consequence that the fit falsified, kept here because withdrawing
> it is the point.** This paragraph used to continue: *"with both households and
> land area inside the index, a coefficient pattern that loads positively on the
> first and negatively on the second is a density preference, expressed without
> any intensive variable entering the model."* **That is not available in this
> model and never was.** The attraction index `beta' a_j` must stay positive for
> its logarithm to exist, which the implementation enforces as
> `beta = exp(theta)`, so no coefficient can be negative. A repelling variable
> cannot be expressed at all; the best it can do is go to the boundary at zero,
> which is exactly what `land_area_sqmi` did. The restriction that buys
> boundary-invariance also forbids the one interpretation that was offered as
> repayment for it. The sentence was written before the code and removed after
> it.

Distance to the nearest existing station is the variable a siting model most
obviously wants, and it is not extensive. Rather than smuggle it in, the main
specification omits it and a pre-registered sensitivity fits it outside the log,
reported alongside. If it moves the coefficients materially, that measures what
the invariance restriction costs, which is worth more than either model alone.

**Three parameters, not five, and two periods, not thirty-two.** Both cuts are
made for power and the document says so. In particular, Train's §7.7.3 advice to
define as few time periods as possible is advice about the computational
tractability of a *forward-looking dynamic programme*; we are fitting a static
conditional logit, so that justification does not transfer and citing it would be
using the right words for the wrong reason. What §7.7.3 legitimately supplies is
the principle that period boundaries should be structural rather than calendar,
which is why the boundary sits at a break in the build-out regime rather than at
an arbitrary midpoint.

covariate apart from one more household.

**Three readings, and none of them is favourable.**

*The pre-registration was confirmed.* §3.2 predicted that the intervals would
cover the null on every coefficient, and said that predicting it in advance is
what would make the eventual report credible rather than a rationalisation. They
do. At p = 0.287 the single covariate that does any work in this model is not
distinguishable from one more household. That is consistent with the held-out
ranking result rather than a second, separate failure: a covariate whose ratio
cannot be told apart from the numeraire is exactly the covariate you would
expect to add nothing over a raw count.

*The sandwich is unavailable for two of three parameters, and that is correct
rather than a shortfall.* `beta = exp(theta)` keeps the attraction index
positive, so `beta = 0` means `theta -> -inf`. A sandwich estimator is derived
from the asymptotic normality of the score at an *interior* maximum where the
gradient vanishes (Train §8.6, p. 201). At a boundary it does not vanish, the
Hessian block is not the information matrix the asymptotics assume, and a
standard error computed there would not be a wide number — it would be a
meaningless one. The implementation refuses to print one and prints the reason.
So the two halves of §6.3's prescription are not equally available, and §6.3
asks for both side by side without noticing that.

*The two resampling units disagree by a factor of two, and the wider one is the
one to quote.* The bootstrap over decisions gives an upper endpoint of 4.76; the
bootstrap over metros gives 10.01. Seven of the 100 loaded facilities are in Los
Angeles and face one choice set with one set of attraction values, so treating
them as seven draws rather than one flatters the interval. The metro-clustered
figure is the conservative one. This is the clustering lesson of §5.3 appearing
a third time, one level further up, and §6.3 did not specify it.

with *which of our 56* are included. That is a real and useful quantity. It is
not the sampling variability of a draw from the population of siting decisions,
and the artefact carries the quotation saying so.

### 5.5 Cost to serve, and depot placement

Cost per delivered package decomposes into a line-haul term from the nearest
depot to the zone and a local term within it, the latter scaling as the inverse
square root of delivery density per Daganzo (1984). The baseline artefact
(`outputs/metrics/cost_report.json`, regenerated 2026-09-16, run
`20260916-064133-4d65`) reports 2,333 areas at a median of **$1.0830** per
parcel, with a tenth percentile of $0.9778 and a ninetieth of $1.4180, over a
solved 334-depot network carrying 13,152,992 daily parcels at a total daily
cost of $14,001,626. An earlier draft of this section quoted $1.0875 from run
`20260913-151310-2940`; that run predated the depot-placement change and is
superseded, and the run identifier `20260914-002418-0623` that this section
carried until 2026-09-16 names a file that no longer exists in that form. The
regeneration was a re-run of the same model on the same inputs: **every figure
above is unchanged**, and the only thing the new run restores is the four
non-baseline scenarios, which the intervening copy of the artefact had dropped.

**The five scenarios, because a single median with no spread beside it is not a
cost model.** All five run end to end over the same 2,333 ZCTAs.

**And the decomposition says where the money actually goes**, recomputed from

influential number in the model. Any figure quoting shares that do not sum to

**Depot placement changed from k-means to a p-median formulation, and this is the
one methodological change in v5 that is a genuine improvement rather than a
retraction.** The reason is an objective mismatch that had been sitting in plain
sight: the cost function bills line haul *linearly* in distance, and k-means
minimises the *square* of distance. It was optimising a different objective from
the one being billed — the weighted-mean-versus-weighted-median error. Klose and
Drexl's taxonomy places the correct model exactly: a minsum objective, a
candidate set equal to the demand points, no fixed cost per depot to trade
against distance, and the depot count fixed exogenously. That is their p-median,
equations (1a)-(1e), with the count set by their aggregate-capacity constraint
(6) rather than the per-depot constraint (11).

**Measured effect of the change**, pilot frame, baseline parameters:

```
                            k-means      p-median      change
  ----------------------------------------------------------
  depots                        334           334          0
  billed parcel-miles    36,552,461    33,156,338     -9.29%
  median $/parcel            1.0860        1.0811      -0.45%
  p90 line-haul miles       10.1426       12.4387     +22.64%

  Spearman rank correlation between the two orderings      0.888
  overlap of the cheapest fifty areas                     22/50
```

The two rows to read together are the 9.29% fall in billed parcel-miles and the
22.64% rise in the ninetieth percentile of raw line haul. They are not in
conflict: the p-median objective is demand-weighted, so depots move towards
volume, high-volume areas get closer and thin ones get further. The quantity the
network actually pays for improves by 9%; the unweighted distribution of distance
gets worse. That is correct behaviour and it is also more realistic. A rank
correlation of 0.888 means correcting the objective moved the ordering about as
much as the single most rank-sensitive parameter in the model does across its
entire plausible range, which is worth knowing before anybody treats the ranking
as stable.

**On the licence to choose depots from a finite list, stated more weakly than it
usually is.** Hakimi (1964) proves that an absolute median of a weighted graph is
always at a vertex, which is the foundation of the whole discrete-location
literature. It does not transfer to our case cleanly, for two reasons we state
rather than elide. The theorem is for **one** facility — there is no `p` anywhere
in the paper, and the p-facility result is a separate 1965 paper we have not
obtained. And its engine is the assumption that any path out of a candidate point
exits through one of the two ends of an edge, which is a statement about a
**graph**; we measure great-circle distance in the plane, where the corresponding
object is a Weber point and is in general not at a demand point.

So the restriction is defended empirically instead. Taking the discrete p-median
solution and letting depots move freely in the plane, alternating nearest-depot
assignment with a continuous Weber solve until nothing moves:

```
  discrete p-median, candidates = area internal points    33,156,338
  continuous multi-source Weber refinement                33,006,455
  cost of the node restriction                                 0.45%
```

Honestly, the continuous figure is itself only a local optimum of a non-convex
problem reached by descending from the discrete solution, so 0.45% is a lower
bound on the true gap. It is still the right order of magnitude, and the
conclusion to state is: *the node restriction is not licensed by Hakimi in our
metric, but it is nearly free, and it buys the guarantee that every depot stands
on an inhabited area rather than at an invented coordinate in a reservoir.* That
guarantee is enforced by a test rather than by intention.

**What the p-median relaxation gives up, measured rather than waved away.** Our
depots have a throughput and we do not enforce it per depot. Assigning every area
to its nearest depot and totalling the load, 139 of 334 depots exceed the assumed
throughput and 20.6% of demand sits above the capacity of the depot nearest to
it. Because a capacity-feasible network must push overflow to a more distant
depot, **our line haul is a lower bound and the model understates cost precisely
where demand is bunched — which is the top of the published ranking.** That is a
directional bias in the headline, not a symmetric error, and it is the first
thing a reviewer should push on. Two honest readings sit beside each other: the
relaxation is substantial rather than technical, and the change to p-median
nevertheless reduces the infeasibility on every measure (worst overload from
4.77x to 3.56x, excess demand from 28.8% to 20.6%).

### 5.6 Portfolio selection, and a defect in its objective

Ranking areas independently assumes their net present values add, and they do
not. Spillovers make adjacent activations worth less together; shared depots and
pooled drop density make them worth more. Two areas each unprofitable alone can
be jointly profitable. We therefore solve a bundle-selection problem by greedy
construction with local search, and report the gap against pure greedy ranking
and against an upper bound. Because spillovers produce diminishing returns while
density economics produces increasing returns, the objective is neither
submodular nor supermodular and the standard greedy bound does not apply, which
makes the size of that gap an empirical question rather than a theorem.

Multi-facility optimisation is not claimed as a contribution; it is a mature
commercial category. It is here because ranking is the wrong model of the
decision, and correcting that is a matter of validity rather than novelty.

Value is reported as a **break-even contribution margin** rather than as a
profit, because margin is unobservable in public data. A break-even margin can be
compared against whatever figure an operator actually has, and asserts nothing we
cannot see.

**A known defect in the objective, stated in the proposal because it shows

The optimiser does not have a single definition of what an activation is. Three

Lines 164 and 158 agree with each other and both contradict line 81. The measured
consequence is a capital overcharge: covering the funded set needs about 103
stations — the per-metro maximum of a greedy fifteen-mile disk cover at 64 and a

naive arithmetic that says "39 areas per station, so this is seven stations" is
wrong by about fourteenfold, because the funded set is a scattered subset of the
cheapest areas across eleven metros rather than a contiguous blob, and because
throughput binds before geometry does.

Two things make this worth a section rather than a footnote. First, **the finding
does not invert the headline**: the budget was already slack, and correcting the
capital unit changes the size of the capital line rather than converting a
binding constraint into a non-binding one. Second, and more usefully, **it is the
same disease that killed the hazard model, in a different component.** The hazard
model failed because area-quarters were not independent decisions; the optimiser
mis-prices because an activation is simultaneously an area, a facility and a
catchment. Deciding what an activation *is* fixes both the capital unit and the
spillover parameter at once, and tuning the two constants separately cannot.

That second point has a consequence for the spillover parameter that the
literature settles and our documents had not. Holmes measures diversion between
outlets of the same chain, where a consumer picks one store from a choice set;
Houde, Newberry and Seim measure the corresponding quantity for fulfilment
centres and find, under a uniform tax, that opening three facilities changes
system-wide order volume by exactly zero — every facility-level decline is
reallocation. Our demand unit is a residential area whose parcels are generated
by the households living in it. Those households do not order fewer parcels
because a neighbouring area was activated, and there is no second outlet for the
volume to move to. So the parameter as currently specified is measuring something
the model does not contain, and the fix is definitional rather than a
recalibration. This is not a small matter: moving it from its current value to
zero changes 43% of the selected portfolio.

### 5.7 The agent layer and the six gates

The agent implements the ReAct pattern with three tools exposed through the Model
Context Protocol: a query generator and a spatial visualiser, both read-only, and
a warehouse mutator that parses competitor infrastructure text, extracts a
facility record and writes it, triggering downstream re-estimation.

**Figure 9.** The agent loop and the six-gate mutation path. Gates 5 and 6 are
the contribution. (figure: docs/figures/fig03_agent_gates.png)

Gates 1 through 4 — schema conformance, geocoding reachability, a confidence
threshold and audit-log immutability — protect data integrity and have prior art
(§2.7). They are not sufficient. Consider a press release announcing a competitor
sortation centre. The extracted record is schema-valid, the coordinates resolve,
confidence is high and the write is logged: all four gates pass. Yet the write
changes which areas are treated as neighbours, which changes the comparison set
for areas in that metro, which moves a downstream estimate and with it the
ranking. The data is correct and the inference is broken.

- **Gate 5 — comparison-set integrity.** Does this mutation move a unit currently
  serving as a control into a treated or contaminated state? If so, the
  comparison set is recomputed and the affected areas flagged, never silently
  re-weighted.
- **Gate 6 — estimate stability.** The affected estimate is recomputed with and
  without the mutation. If the change exceeds a pre-registered threshold, the
  mutation escalates to human review regardless of automation settings, and the
  delta is surfaced in the diff card.

All six run against the real warehouse today and write an audit record per
invocation. Gate 5 escalates often, because the comparison pool is thin — which
is itself a signal about the data rather than a defect in the gate. For any
public demonstration, human-in-the-loop confirmation is enabled by default: every
mutation that clears all six gates still requires an explicit human approval
before the write lands.

The adversarial evaluation that would turn "the gates run" into "the gates work"
is **not built**, and §3.6 says so in the same words.

### 5.9 Engineering feasibility and reproducibility

The analytical panel is 1,081,312 rows and 14.6 MB, and the warehouse compresses
to a single portable file. This is deliberate. The hard problem is
identification, not throughput, and no distributed compute appears anywhere in
the design. **Row count is the only dimension on which this project is small**,
and the proposal does not present it as a strength or apologise for it.

The one component that does not fit that description is road-network
preprocessing. A full-US extract requires memory well beyond a laptop, and
retaining processed artefacts for ten metros simultaneously would consume tens of
gigabytes. The design therefore removes routing from the critical path entirely:
per metro, the road graph is processed offline, the drive-time matrix extracted,
and every intermediate artefact deleted before the next metro begins, so the
deployed application reads a single small lookup with no routing dependency at
all. **That matrix is designed and not built.** Distance today is great-circle
scaled by a circuity factor, and the sensitivity sweep shows that factor moves
the median cost per parcel by about one per cent across its plausible range — so
this is precision in the right place being deferred, not a hole.

Reproducibility is enforced by three mechanisms rather than asserted. Every
headline figure is derived into `outputs/metrics/` with a run identifier and read
from there by the figure and document builders, which raise at import if the
artefact is missing, so a figure cannot be drawn with an invented number. The
test suite is **663 collected, 661 passing, 2 expected failures, 0 failures**,
measured on 2026-09-16 by `pytest -q -p no:randomly`, exit code 0. This
document previously said 648 with 2 expected failures at commit `b29071e` on
2026-09-14; `docs/NUMBERS.md` records 660 collected and 658 passing earlier on
2026-09-16. All three are correct measurements of the tree in front of the
person taking them, which is the point the next paragraph makes.

**One thing must be quoted with that number and never without it.** An AST
import-graph traversal seeded from every file under `tests/` reaches **63 of
the 138 source modules** in `src/siting_atlas/`; **75 are unreachable from any
test**. Every artefact behind the results in §3.3, §5.3, §5.10 and §5.11 is
written by a module in the unreachable list. *"The tests pass"* and *"the
headline artefacts have no test importing their emitter"* are both true, and a
reader who is given only the first has been misled by omission.

That figure carries a date for a reason. Over roughly two hours on the day this
was written the suite passed through five different states — 638 passing, then

the project's most serious operational risk. That claim has been removed because

The discipline is imperfect in two respects worth naming, and the second was

*The prose is not derived.* Every number in this document was typed by a human

*The two Brier scores are not comparable.* 0.007550 against 0.005036 looks like a

strictly earlier than their opening

drawn from the better-documented end of the list.

boundary.* On the 104-row fit both `land_area_sqmi` and `establishments` sat at

spread [0.022, 0.712] over re-splits, while `land_area_sqmi` stays at the

parameter becomes estimable — and it is also the clearest evidence that the
first fit's boundary solutions were a sample-size artefact rather than a
statement about land area. It does not move the ranking, which is the point of
this subsection.

The frame's statistical power, on the other hand, is no longer in doubt.
Restricted to the panel window, the expanded frame holds 440 decisions across 378
independent (CBSA, quarter) episodes, which at five parameters is **75.6 events
per parameter against 15.8** on the 104-row file and a conventional floor of ten.
That figure is **projected, not fitted** — the artefact labels it so — and it
compares two frames that both already cleared the floor. Clearing a power floor
by a wider margin is not a result, and §3.1's argument applies here unchanged.

#### 5.10.2 An aggregation artefact, recorded because it nearly produced the wrong conclusion

An earlier reading of the pooled figures suggested performance had *fallen*. It
had not. The expanded data is weighted toward small markets — decisions with
fewer than 25 candidate ZCTAs roughly doubled as a share of the sample — and **in
a choice set that small, random guessing already lands in the top ten about
two-thirds of the time**, so the maximum achievable lift is capped near 1.5x
regardless of model quality. Averaging across markets of very different
difficulty measures the mix of questions, not the model.

The consequence is a reporting rule rather than a result: **pooled top-k across
heterogeneous choice sets is not an interpretable statistic for this problem**,
and results are reported stratified by market size. Simpson's paradox is the
textbook name for it and this is a clean instance of it.

**The stratified numbers can now be quoted, and they land exactly where the
argument above predicted.** Until 2026-09-15 this paragraph refused to give
them, because the only artefact carrying them —
`outputs/metrics/lift_by_market_size.json` — is the one file in the project
that **no code in the tree can regenerate**: several citations, zero writers, no
run identifier, and a summary string that contradicts its own fields. That
refusal stands and the artefact is formally retired; its circulating figures of
6.14 and 6.42 must not be quoted by anything. What replaced it is
`outputs/metrics/panel_experiments.json`, which is emitted by code, holds five
panel compositions against three algorithms, and stratifies by choice-set size
with the distinct-decision count printed beside every cell.

```

  25 or fewer            65        96.8%       66.4%    1.46x

  more than 100         258        30.3%        4.8%    6.26x

Read the top row. **In a choice set of 25 or fewer, random guessing is already
in the top ten two thirds of the time**, so the ceiling on lift there is about

stratum. Averaging the three rows measures the mix of markets, not the model.
The large stratum is the only one where a siting decision is genuinely
contested, and it is the one the rest of this document quotes.

The same table across the five panel compositions is what settles the

lift moved by 0.08.** Naming the arm, the method and the artefact every time is
not pedantry here: four different artefacts in this project publish a quantity
called "large-metro top-10 lift", on four different frames, with different
nulls and different numbers of splits.

#### 5.10.3 The audit the new dates made possible

This is the part that is genuinely novel, and it is an audit of the project's own
key covariate rather than of anyone else's.

§6 defect M is the charge: an Amazon delivery station **is** a warehousing
establishment, so a contemporaneous industry count of warehousing establishments
would contain its own outcome. The guard against it requires industry data
measured before the facility's opening year, and §5.4 now shows the guard is
defeated by a median 34 months over n = 42.

**First, the stakes.** Removing the covariate costs **7.70 of 38** held-out hits
and loses on **0 of 50** re-splits (`outputs/metrics/leakage_test.json`). The
model is substantially this one column, so the question is not academic.

**Second, the decisive test.** Refit using, per facility, industry data from
strictly before its **stated** opening date — which is possible only because the
dates of §4.8 exist. `outputs/metrics/leakage_decisive.json`, 50 re-splits, seed
20260914, 29 decisions, 12 held out:

```
  contaminated vintage (the OSHA bound)     9.24 of 12    sd 1.00
  honest vintage (the stated date)          8.22 of 12    sd 1.04
  covariate removed entirely                4.48 of 12    sd 1.25

  share of the covariate's value retained under honest vintages   79%
```

**Third, a model-free check that agrees in the mean and disagrees in the
median.** Between the two vintages the chosen ZCTA gains **+1.04 warehouses more**
than its rival ZCTAs in the same metro — almost exactly one building, which is
what self-counting predicts. But the median excess is **zero** and only **11 of
29** facilities show any (sd 2.56).

**The conclusion, and it survives.** The covariate is mostly genuine
agglomeration, with a self-counting component affecting roughly a third of
facilities and worth at most a fifth of its predictive value.

**The caveats are not decoration and the artefact states them itself.**
Requiring both a stated date and a clean industry vintage cut the sample to 29
decisions — 69% of the working set is gone, and the survivors are the buildings
MWPVL happens to list and the address matcher happens to reach, which is an
easier subsample than the full panel. More seriously, the honest arm reads a
vintage a median two years *older* as well as a cleaner one, so staleness and
contamination are the same intervention here and **the 21% figure is an upper
bound on the leak, not a measurement of it**. Two stated dates are not earlier
than the OSHA bound for the same building and were used as stated rather than
corrected.

#### 5.10.4 Two independent lines now point at the same ceiling

```

  2  quintupling the sample changed ranking accuracy not at all      Sec. 5.10.1

Neither is a failure of execution and neither is rescued by more of the same

It also points at the most promising remaining move, and §6 does not yet carry it
as a defect because it is a hypothesis rather than a fault. In a large metro the
model ranks 300 or more candidate ZCTAs, most of which are physically incapable
of hosting a 150,000 square foot building. The model does not know this. An
independent finding is consistent with it: of the 43 pilot facilities ranked by
cost-to-serve **within their own metro**, **zero** fall in their metro's
cheapest decile against a chance rate of 10%, 5 of 43 (11.6%) fall in the

which the coverage gap of §3.3, the audited covariate above, and the withdrawal

the ceiling, the prediction result stands on its own. If it does not, the ceiling
itself — quantified across several methods and covariate sets — is the finding,
and it is a more defensible one than a model that merely worked.

### 5.11 The metro-entry model, pre-registered before it was fitted

*New on 2026-09-15, and placed at the end of §5 for the same reason §4.8 and
§5.10 sit where they do: nothing above it had to be renumbered. It is the
fourth negative finding in this document and the only one that was committed to
in writing, in a hashed file, before the answer was known.*

#### 5.11.1 Why the question needed pre-registering, and what was fixed

Every failure reported above is a failure *within* a metropolitan area. §5.3
could not rank ZIP-code areas inside a metro; §5.4 and §5.10 could rank them
only as well as one free Census column. The obvious remaining hypothesis is
that the **grain** was wrong rather than the data — that a great deal of US
public data is published at county level or coarser, and that a within-metro
model therefore throws away exactly the variation those columns carry. The
mechanism predicts the fix: variation that is *between counties* is, by
construction, *between metros*.

That hypothesis had three properties that together made it dangerous. It was
appealing; it was the project's own diagnosis pointing at its own escape route;
and there was nothing left after it. A project that has published three
negative results and has one hypothesis remaining has every incentive to choose
a framing after seeing the answer.

So the framing was fixed first, written out in full and hashed.

**Four details sharpen that, and each one closes an escape route.**

*The uncertainty is not marginal.* A metro-clustered bootstrap — 2,000 draws
over 935 CBSA clusters, seed 20260913 — puts the model's AUC at 0.7319
[0.6876, 0.7706] and **model minus households at -0.1628 [-0.2011, -0.1313]**.

only one anywhere in §5.10 or §5.11; every other bracket in those two sections
is a percentile spread over re-splits of one fixed set (§6 defect R). The
secondary evaluation agrees: over 50 re-splits with 280 held-out metros, the
model loses to households on paired AUC in **50 of 50**, mean difference
-0.175.

*The fitted model reproduces a column of integers.* Pooled over the seven
held-out years, a two-covariate logistic fitted seven times scores 0.7323;
baseline 3 — the zero-parameter count of facilities already open in the metro —
scores **0.7325** on the same 6,545 rows. The model is not merely beaten by the
better baseline, it is indistinguishable from the worse one.

*Stratifying strengthens the finding rather than rescuing it.* Split the 935
metros into terciles of households, fixed across years, and the model scores
0.4125 / 0.5666 / 0.6961 against a households baseline of 0.7668 / 0.6827 /
0.8023, on 6 / 27 / 273 events. **The pooled AUC of 0.7323 is higher than the
model's AUC in every single tier** — the textbook aggregation effect, because
size both predicts the outcome and defines the strata. Quoted alone, 0.73 would
have read as a working model. In the smallest tercile the model scores below a
coin flip; six events is far too few to make anything of that and we make
nothing of it, but reporting the pooled figure alone would have concealed it.

*Leakage is not what is holding the model up.* The diagnostic `vintage_relaxed`
arm — seven covariates including households itself, permitted to see data
published after the year it is predicting — still loses to ranking on
households alone in **6 of 7** years, its single win being +0.0004 of AUC, and
loses calibration **7 of 7**. Letting the model cheat does not close the gap.
The ceiling is the baseline.

**The single largest qualification on this run belongs here and not in a
footnote.** The registered vintage rule removed **8 of the 12 pre-registered
covariates** and the coverage floor removed two more, leaving the verdict arm
with two: `facilities_open_prior` and `dist_nearest_outside_prior`. The reason
is structural rather than incidental. Most of the panel's columns are not time
series at all — mean distinct values per unit across 32 quarters is exactly
1.00 for population, households, income, both EJScreen columns, both wage
series and metro employment. They are single-vintage broadcasts from ACS 2023,
BLS OES May 2025 and EJScreen 2024, all of which post-date most of the held-out
years. **The Tier 1 mechanism that generated H1 was therefore never testable on
this panel.** That is a finding about data availability rather than about the
hypothesis, and it points at the same conclusion from a different direction:
the covariates that might have distinguished one metro from another are
published once, late, and without a usable history.

One deviation runs the other way and is recorded because it is conservative for
the conclusion reached. Baseline 2 is scored on the same ACS 2023 households
column that the vintage gate rules **inadmissible** as a covariate for 2019-2023.
The two cannot both be honoured with the single ACS vintage on disk, and
dropping the baseline would leave the criterion with nothing to test against, so
the pre-registration's baseline was run as written. The bar is therefore set
with information the model is not allowed to see — which biases the comparison
*against* H1, and means a narrow H1 win under this arrangement would not have
been safe to report. A loss of 0.16 of AUC is not narrow.

#### 5.11.3 Why a covariate fails: within-metro dispersion

The pre-registration's motivating mechanism deserves its own statement, because
it is the most transferable thing in this section and because the version the
pre-registration used was wrong.

A conditional choice model differences away everything that is constant within
a choice set. A covariate's only currency is therefore how much it varies among
the roughly two hundred candidate areas of one metro. Measured directly on the
frame: the mean number of distinct values per large metro is about 200 for land
area and 193 for households, but **9.2** for the EJScreen and ACS county-grain
columns and 8.0 and 7.9 for the two building-permit columns. Six of eighteen
columns offer the model nine distinct values with which to rank two hundred
alternatives, and `covariate_search.json`'s `column_audit` marks all six with a
`county_grain_share` of exactly 1.00. That is not a data-quality problem —
`traffic_proximity` is populated on 100% of the metro frame's rows — it is a
publication-grain problem.

Scoring each of the 21 network terms in
`experiments/gravity-network/artefacts/gravity_network.json` (run
`20260915-210603-b780`, `terms.dispersion` at vintage 2030, 41 large metros of
101 or more candidates, 8,271 candidate ZCTAs) by its mean within-metro
coefficient of variation, against whether its coefficient is interior or at the
boundary in the arm where it is fitted:

```
  cv band        terms   interior   not interior
  ------------------------------------------------
  below 0.6          7          0              7      range 0.2975 - 0.5918
  0.6 to 1.3         0         --             --      THE BAND IS EMPTY
  above 1.3         14          9              5      range 1.3993 - 8.5046
```

**Figure 11.** The same 21 terms plotted one by one rather than banded. Every
value on the image is read from `gravity_network.json` at build time.
(figure: docs/figures/fig_dispersion.png)

**The rule runs one way, and the two-way version was exactly the part that made
it sound like a predictor.**

> Low within-metro dispersion is **sufficient for failure**: all 7 terms with
> cv below 0.6 land at the coefficient boundary. High dispersion is
> **necessary but not sufficient for success**: 9 of the 14 above cv 1.3 are
> interior, and the 5 that are not are all on the sortation side, four of them
> carrying square footage as mass.

Two things raise this above coincidence in the direction it does run. The
gravity exponent is a *knob*: moving it from 1 to 3 changes nothing about what
the covariate measures — still pull from the same facilities — and changes only
how sharply it discriminates within a metro. Across the eighteen gravity terms
the cv runs from **0.30 at exponent 1 to 8.50 at exponent 3**; all six
exponent-1 terms fail and four of the six exponent-3 terms are interior, so the
coefficient walks from the boundary towards the interior as the knob turns
without ever getting all the way there. And it retrospectively
explains a failure that had no explanation: a *count* of facilities within 50
miles sits at cv 0.59 and fails at 25, 50 and 100 miles, while a *distance* to
the same facilities works. The radius was never the issue.

**Read it as description and not as an estimated relationship, and the limits
are not decoration.** n = 21 terms from a single run at a single vintage, and
the terms are **not independent** — each is one of two columns in one of 13
arms fitted over the same 483 decisions. Nothing at all was observed between cv
0.6 and 1.3, so the finding is silent about intermediate dispersion. And *"at
the boundary"* is a **rule, not a fact**: the re-split percentile rule used here
calls `sortation_proximity` boundary-inside-the-interval, while a point-estimate
rule applied to a 500-replicate clustered bootstrap in a different artefact
calls the same column `at_boundary: false`. Both are current and both are right
under their own rule, so the rule is named every time. What this supports is a
**screening heuristic worth seconds of compute before fitting anything** — and
no more than that.

#### 5.11.4 Two neighbouring lines, tested in the same week and not adopted

Both are reported because a section that lists only what worked is not a
record of what was done.

***Gravity instead of proximity: better on three metrics out of four, and not
adopted.*** The natural upgrade to "distance to the nearest existing facility"
is a distance-discounted sum of pull from *all* of them. Thirteen arms were
built over the same 483 decisions, the same alternatives and the same 50
re-splits, so every difference is a model difference
(`gravity_network.json`, run `20260915-210603-b780`).

```
  large-metro top-10 lift
    no_network            the floor, no network term at all      6.2585
    proximity_published   the published baseline                 6.2903
    gravity_count_a3      best arm with BOTH columns interior    6.2187

  gravity_count_a3 vs proximity_published, paired over 50 re-splits
    top-1     +0.0116    42 improved / 4 worsened
    top-5     +0.0126    44 / 2
    top-10    -0.0002    18 / 22
    Brier     better     40 / 10
```

Gravity wins at k = 1, k = 5 and on the proper score, and loses at k = 10 —
where the best fully-interior gravity arm sits **below both** the published
proximity baseline and the no-network floor. There is no single scalar for
"gravity versus proximity"; it depends on k, and any document that quotes one
has chosen a k after seeing the answer. Two things settled the decision against
it. Offered both families at once the fitted model *keeps* gravity and pushes
proximity to the boundary, which is a real measurement in gravity's favour. But
a gravity term is **not extensive**: merging two areas does not add their
attractions, so the zone-merger invariance that §5.4 pays for its entire
functional form — Train §3.4 Example 2 — does not hold for any arm in the file
except `no_network`. Measured over 43,224 merged area pairs, the median
absolute relative probability error is **0.044** and the maximum **0.455** for
the network arm, against **exactly 0.0** for the extensive-only control. Trading
an invariance the specification is built on for +0.012 at top-5 is not a trade
this project is willing to make, and the specification is unchanged.

***White space: retired, but it produced the densification measurement.***
Ranking unserved places by an under-coverage score was tested against the
household baseline over 108 scored cells (`outputs/metrics/white_space.json`).
It wins **14 of 108** (13%), and reaches p < 0.05 in its own favour **0 times**
against **32** for the baseline. It is a real description of who is unserved and
is usable as that; it is not a predictor of where the operator builds next.

What survives is the regularity it exposed on the way. At the headline 45-mile
radius, **68.7% to 75.9%** of 2024-25 delivery-station openings landed inside
coverage the pre-2024 network already had — 75.9% is 60 of 79 openings with
real Census coordinates, 68.7% is 90 of 131 once ZCTA-centroid fallbacks are
admitted. **That range is across coordinate sets at one radius, not across
radii**, and the distinction is not pedantic: 67.1% is the real-coordinate
figure at *15* miles, so the "67-77%" that circulated in this project and in
the pre-registration silently mixed two axes and quoted the range both wider
and looser than the artefact supports. Across the full radius-by-coordinate-set
grid the value runs from 45.0% to 75.9%.

The operator densifies where it already serves. That is the single strongest
regularity the project found, it is the reason the pre-registration's Tier 3
covariates exist, and it did not rescue the model either. In the verdict arm,
which has no size control, `facilities_open_prior` loads positively as expected
(0.42 to 0.74 across the seven years). In the `vintage_relaxed` arm, which is
the only one that also carries households and population, the **same covariate
turns negative in all seven years** (-0.45 to -0.09). Densification is real, and
once metro size is in the model it stops being information: what looks like
"the operator builds where it already builds" is mostly "the operator builds
where the people are", which is the sentence §5.3 retired a model over.

## 6. Known open defects

Listed in the order they would hurt a reader who relied on this work. A proposal
that lists its defects is easier to assess than one that does not, and every item
here is measured rather than suspected.

**A. CLOSED. The repository is under version control.** This entry read "the
repository is not under version control. No history exists, and concurrent edits
are unrecoverable. Not a modelling defect, and the highest operational risk in
the project. Fix first." It was true when written and it has been fixed. The
entry is kept as a closed item rather than deleted, because the numbering is
referenced elsewhere in this document.

**B. The optimiser does not have a single definition of an activation.** Three
lines price the same object as an area, as a facility and as a facility with a
catchment. Measured consequence: a 2.74x capital overcharge. Full statement in
§5.6. It is the same unit-of-analysis error that killed the hazard model,
occurring in a second component, which is the reason it is listed this high.
**It is now the highest-ranked open defect.**

**C. CLOSED AS STATED, REOPENED AS SOMETHING SHARPER.** This entry read "the
published optimiser and cost artefacts are stale relative to their own source
code". Both have since been regenerated end to end (runs `20260914-002418-0623`
and `20260914-002431-7419`), so the staleness is gone. What the regeneration
exposed is worse than what it fixed: **depot placement moves the headline more
than any documented parameter and is not classified as a parameter.** The
500-draw Monte Carlo in §5.6 puts the superseded 330 at the 97th percentile of
the parameter distribution and the current 282 at the 67th, which is the
measurement that turns "the artefact was stale" into "our register of what
counts as a parameter is incomplete". The register is the defect. Nothing has
been done about it.

**D. Forty-four per cent of PILOT opening quarters are a convention, and the
successor was fitted around the problem rather than through it.** Nineteen of 43
pilot facilities have no recorded quarter and are defaulted to Q1, producing
35.47% of fitted events in Q1 against 25% expected. The flag is carried to the
panel; the values are still unknown. This used to be listed as what blocked
fitting the successor. It no longer blocks it, because the successor was fitted
on the national frame, which reports a quarter for all 104 of its rows — see
defect M for why that is less reassuring than it sounds. The nineteen pilot
quarters remain unresolved, and any result that returns to the pilot frame
inherits them.

**E. Interval censoring is not implemented.** Most dates are "operating by" upper
bounds with verified lags of 4, 13, 57, 69 and 345 months, and the risk set
treats each as an exact event time. The successor specification *reduces* the
exposure, because the date becomes a conditioning variable rather than the event
time, but it does not remove it: a lag of that size would place a decision in the
wrong period or outside the frame. It returns with the timing factor of §5.4's
decomposition, after the choice model exists to attach it to.

**F. PART-CLOSED, AND THE PART THAT CLOSED IS NOT JOINED IN.** All three panels
are 0% populated on latitude and longitude, and coordinates are filled from area
centroids. Every distance quantity in the project inherits that error, and it
gates the distance covariate in §5.4. The count of addresses needing geocoding
rose from 104 to **693** with the expansion of §4.8. The batch submission has
since been made and reaches **72.3%** — 501 Match of 693, 190 No_Match, 2 Tie —
but the result lives in a side file that is **not joined into the panel** and
**not under version control**, and `national_panel_expanded.json` still records
`coordinates_present: 0`. So the defect is unchanged where it bites. A "before"
figure of 65.0% (455 of 700) circulated in earlier drafts; it was measured on
the superseded 700-row panel and **no file reproducing it survives on disk**,
so it should not be quoted as a comparison.

**G. A served-now flag is conflated with an ever-served flag.** The warehouse
takes the earliest opening and the latest closing per area, so a gap between one
station closing and another opening reads as served throughout. Harmless for
first-enablement, wrong for the cost and visualisation layers. Only one closure
exists today, so the blast radius is small and non-zero.

**H. The declared edit set is prose, not a build gate.** Twenty-two constraints
are written out with their measured violation counts; none is enforced. The
cheapest large win is the consistency check over the edit set itself, which runs
before any data is touched — one of the twenty-two is already known to be wrong
as written, because it fires on missingness rather than on contradiction.

**I. No outlier screen exists.** The redesign is specified — declared
plausibility band, then influence ranking, then a four-way diagnosis, with both
thresholds printed beside the count — and is not built. A naive distributional
screen is explicitly *not* the answer: it flags 28.36% of areas and would delete
Manhattan.

**J. Endogeneity is named and not addressed.** Unobserved attributes that make an
area attractive plausibly drive the observed attributes we measure. The control
function is the right tool, costs at minimum one more parameter plus a first
stage on a sample that cannot afford three, and requires an instrument we do not
have. BLP is unavailable for a separate and verified reason: observed shares are
zero for most alternatives in every market.

**K. Independence from irrelevant alternatives is implausible here.** Adjacent
areas are near-perfect substitutes for a siting decision. The natural repair is a
nested logit with metros as nests, which is also structurally the right shape for
§5.4's two-factor decomposition — and each nest adds a parameter, which §3.1
forbids. Recorded as the first extension to attempt if the sample grows.

**L. A load-bearing docstring in the model layer no longer describes the data.**
It asserts six distinct event times all in Q1 and forces a modelling choice on
that basis; the artefact reports seventeen event times across all four quarters.
The choice may still be right; the reason given for it is not. Flagged, not
fixed, because that module serves the retired specification.

**M. The endogeneity guard on the headline covariate is nominal, and this is
the defect that undercuts the headline result.** An Amazon delivery station *is*
a warehousing establishment, so a contemporaneous County Business Patterns count
would contain its own outcome. `ingest/cbp_detail.py` installs a vintage lag
against exactly that, scoring each facility on the latest CBP vintage strictly
earlier than its recorded `open_year`. The guard is correct in design and
defeated in practice, because `open_year` is not an opening date: on **all 100
loaded national rows**, `open_year` and `open_quarter` equal the quarter of the
earliest OSHA inspection. It is the "operating by" upper bound, restated. A
building first inspected in 2022 may have opened in 2017, in which case the
"strictly earlier" 2021 vintage already counts the facility itself. Its true
margin is zero or negative rather than one year. Listed here rather than
beneath §5.4 because the covariate it undercuts,
`warehousing_establishments`, is the only one in the model that does any work.

**PART-CLOSED 2026-09-14. The defect is unchanged; its size is now measured and
its consequence is smaller than its severity.** This entry used to rest on five
addresses with lags of 4, 13, 57, 69 and 345 months. The recovered opening dates
of §4.8 put the gap at a **median 34 months over n = 42**, with 88% of gaps
exceeding a year — so the guard is defeated more thoroughly than the five
addresses suggested. But the same dates make the decisive test possible for the
first time, and §5.10 reports it: refitting on industry data from strictly
before each facility's *stated* opening retains **79% of the covariate's
predictive value** (8.22 against 9.24 of 12 held-out hits, against 4.48 with the
covariate removed), on 29 decisions. The covariate is mostly genuine
agglomeration. What remains open is the fix, not the measurement.

**N. CLOSED, AND THE ANSWER IS NEGATIVE. The choice model's intervals now
exist and they cover the null.** This entry read "the choice model has no
standard errors and no intervals, and the specification prescribes both". It was
true for most of 2026-09-14 and was fixed the same day: `choice_report.json`
carries an `inference` block with a sandwich covariance, a bootstrap over
decisions, a bootstrap over metros and a BCa interval. On the only interior
parameter the sandwich gives [0.734, 2.835] against a null of 1.0 at p = 0.287,
and all four intervals cover 1.0 (§5.4). The sandwich is correctly refused for
the two boundary parameters. **The defect is closed and what it revealed is
that the coefficient is not distinguishable from one more household**, which is
the result §3.2 pre-registered. One sub-item remains open: the decision-level
and metro-level bootstraps disagree by a factor of two on the upper endpoint,
and `MODEL_SPEC.md` §6.3 does not say which to quote. It should say the
metro-clustered one.

**O. Satellite dating was attempted and failed, and the failure is unfixed.**
36% of estimates date construction after the day OSHA found the building
operating, and the confidence score does not separate those from the rest
(§4.3). The cause is localised — the changepoint detector, not the imagery — so
the route is not closed, only unsuccessful so far.

This entry used to continue: *"Because the timing factor of §5.4's decomposition
needs a lower bound on opening dates, and satellite imagery was the route to
one, this failure also keeps defects E and M open."* **That clause is
withdrawn.** Satellite was *a* route and not *the* route. A different route
worked on the same day and supplied 1,420 stated opening dates (§4.8), which
passed the identical logical test at 93.6% where the satellite method passed it
at 64%. Defects E and M are no longer blocked by this failure; defect M is
part-closed on the strength of it. The satellite result stands as a negative
finding on its own terms.

**P. The labelling programme was generated against the wrong reference.** 289 of
362 worklist rows (80%) were already classified in a file the generator never
consulted (§4.3). The programme is complete and the bug is unfixed in
`scripts/make_unlabelled_batches.py`, so a seventh batch generated today would
repeat it. One figure from that programme needs a warning attached wherever it

**R. NEW. There is no inference on the expanded panel.** The sandwich
covariance, the bootstrap over decisions, the bootstrap over metros, the BCa
interval and the conformal prediction sets were all computed on the 104-row
pilot panel, and not one of them has been re-run on the 693-row one. Every
bracket this document quotes from `panel_experiments.json`,
`refit_expanded.json`, `gravity_network.json` or the re-split block of
`metro_entry.json` is a percentile over 50 re-splits of one fixed decision set,
not a standard error and not a confidence interval; the artefacts carry a
`spread_is_not_a_standard_error` field saying so and this document labels them
accordingly. **The project has the better sample and the worse inference on it.**
The single exception is the metro-clustered bootstrap of §5.11, which is a
genuine bootstrap interval over 935 clusters. Ranked second in §9.

**S. NEW, AND FOUND IN SELF-AUDIT. Two figures carried hand-typed numbers
presented as measurement, and the rule that should have caught them lived in a
docstring.** `fig05_decay` drew a band labelled "95% CI" and an "n.s."
annotation over nine typed values, on an outcome variable that does not exist
in this project's data; `fig07_tornado` drew eight typed bucket swings with no
disclosure at all. Both were found on 2026-09-15 and de-quantified the same day,
and neither is embedded in this document. §5.9 gives the full account and
`docs/data/FIGURES.md` gives the code. The defect is recorded as closed on those
two figures and **open as a class**: ten of the fourteen numbered figures still
carry no artefact binding, and the rule that a figure must read every value from
an artefact or carry none is enforced by `tools/figures/fig_paper.py`'s
convention rather than by a test.

## 8. Roles and Contributions

Roles are defined by module ownership **and** by pipeline stage. Assigning modules
without assigning stages is how a shared pipeline becomes nobody's
responsibility. All deliverables are jointly reviewed before submission.

**P1 — technical direction, identification, integration.** Warehouse and
transformation models; the siting specification and its estimation; the portfolio
optimiser; the agent and its tools; the identification gates. Owns the answer to
*"how do you know this is a decision and not a consequence?"* — which, on the
evidence of §5.3, is the question this project exists to have got wrong once and
then right.

**P2 — data sources, validation, reproducibility.** Ingest for every public
source; the facility panel and its provenance; the data-quality audit and the
sentinel registry; the coverage estimate of §3.3; data contracts, the test suite,
environment pinning and seed management. Owns the answer to *"does it build from
cold, in one command?"* and, newly, *"how much of the footprint do you actually
see?"*

**P3 — delivery, visualisation, communication.** Application build-out; operator
and public planning views; the figure toolkit; the equity overlay; the executive
deck; the write-up series and the open-dataset release with its data card. Owns
the answer to *"what decision does someone actually make?"*

**Joint.** Proposal submission, midterm review, final deployment and the
executive presentation require sign-off from all three contributors. One
additional joint responsibility is new in v5 and is a direct consequence of §5.6:
**no figure enters any deliverable unless it is read from an artefact or is a
schematic with no numbers in it.** Four figures were removed from this document
under that rule.

**That rule was then tested by the project's own audit and found not to have
been enforced.** On 2026-09-15 two figures in the wider set were found to carry
hand-typed numbers presented as measurement — one of them a fabricated 95%
confidence band and a significance annotation on a variable this project does
not observe. Neither was in this document, both were de-quantified the same day,
and the full disclosure is in §5.9 and §6 defect S. The lesson recorded here is
narrower than "be careful": the rule existed, it was written down, and it lived
in a module docstring where nothing could enforce it. A rule with no gate behind
it is a preference.

## 10. Budget

Compute cost is zero. Every data source is free and the entire pipeline runs on a
laptop; the deliberate absence of distributed compute (§5.9) is a budget decision
as well as a design one.

The entire budget is language-model inference for the agent layer, and it is
bounded in configuration rather than by intention: cumulative spend is logged per
call with token counts, exposed in the application, and a guard raises a
controlled exception before the cap, forcing an explicit human decision to draw
on the contingency. The figure sits inside the course's hard cap, and an earlier
draft that contained two incompatible budgets has been resolved to one.

Two components a reader might expect are out of scope and recorded as such: a
fine-tuned narration model, and a multi-model language-model ablation. Neither
contributes to the contributions in §5.8, and the ablation would be underpowered
at any sample size this project can afford — which is, at this point, a familiar
sentence.
